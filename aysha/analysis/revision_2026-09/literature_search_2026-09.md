# Systematic search, September 2026: measurement adequacy and flow maldistribution

Two questions were put to the literature. The first is whether the recent experimental record on volumetric solar receivers and on monolith channels supports the manuscript's claim in §5.1 that campaigns are instrumented too sparsely — steady state, gas outlet plus a surface temperature — to constrain the internal field or to identify a transient thermal capacitance. The second is whether a 2023–2026 reference exists that would strengthen §5.2, where the argument turns on the channel-to-channel flow split being a live degree of freedom that a single-stream model does not possess. The answer to the first is that the claim holds with one important qualification: spatial resolution has advanced substantially, but on the front surface rather than inside the absorber. The answer to the second is yes, and there are three distinct references worth adding.

## What recent receiver experiments measure

The 2019–2026 experimental record on porous and structured volumetric absorbers is still organised around thermal efficiency and air outlet temperature as the reported observables, which is the pattern §5.1 asserts. Patil and co-workers characterise a 5 kW reticulated-porous-ceramic cavity receiver through "the thermal efficiency and the air outlet temperature" ([Patil et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.solener.2020.11.045)), and the heat-transfer analysis of the same receiver class carries the same observables forward ([Patil and Steinfeld 2024](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.4066499)). At DLR's Synlight facility, Broeske and co-workers test three-dimensionally shaped absorbers in a sixteen-module matrix against an extruded SiSiC honeycomb monolith of the HiTRec type — the same material and structure class as this receiver — at up to 650 kW m⁻² and air exit temperatures to 735 °C, reaching thermal efficiencies of 91.8% at 650 °C ([Broeske et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.renene.2023.119220)). Their measurement set is thermocouples placed directly behind each of the sixteen positions for air exit temperature, an infrared camera for surface emission, and a spectrometer for solar-weighted absorptivity; efficiency follows from a radiative energy balance. The reason they give for that choice is the single most useful sentence this search turned up: individual mass flow measurements for each absorber were impossible due to space constraints, so the efficiency was evaluated radiatively rather than via the air enthalpy flow. The field's most advanced recent honeycomb campaign therefore has neither an internal solid temperature nor a per-module flow rate, by its authors' own statement. The CAPTure open volumetric air receiver was validated at 15 kW on a solar simulator and at 300 kW on an experimental tower, reported through absorber-module outlet temperature above 900 °C ([Zaversky et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.52825/solarpaces.v1i.713)). Where the instrumentation is better, it is better on the enthalpy side rather than the internal-field side: the pressurised-gas receiver campaign of D'Souza and co-workers uses a high-flux solar simulator with a calorimetric test setup ([D'Souza et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applthermaleng.2025.125634)), which is precisely the independent outlet-enthalpy determination §5.3 ranks second in value. Absorber selection studies proceed by fitting effective parameters to the same class of measurement rather than by resolving the field ([Arreola-Ramos et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/en14092506)).

The qualification that should be written into §5.1 is that dynamic, spatially resolved validation is now being done, and that the observable is the front surface. Iding and co-workers present a real-time capable simulation of open volumetric receiver surface temperatures with high spatial resolution, validated against data recorded at the solar tower Jülich, where the receiver comprises 1080 absorber cups and each cup is discretised into a heating zone and a transport zone ([Iding et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.52825/solarpaces.v1i.885)). The two validated observables are the front temperature and the air temperature directly behind the cup, and the model reproduces them to root-mean-square errors of 6.78 K and 7.51 K respectively — an excellent fit on exactly the two-quantity dataset §5.1 argues cannot discriminate among receiver models. The paper is candid that the per-cup performance does not carry to the array: simulating the whole receiver requires an advanced optimisation of the model parameters. This is a sharper version of the manuscript's point than a bare absence would be, and the array-versus-cup discrepancy is itself circumstantial support for §5.2, since what a per-cup model cannot know is how the flow divides among the cups. The field can already resolve the surface in space and in time, and it still cannot resolve depth, which is exactly where §5.1 shows the identified coefficients to be determined — the front-wall-only and rear-wall-only rows of the reference-choice table differ by a factor of 28.9 in the Nusselt prefactor.

Transient receiver work exists and is predominantly computational. Sharma and Talukdar use a coupled transient model to obtain solid and fluid temperature fields, outlet fluid temperature and pressure drop through start-up, shut-down, clear sky and cloud passage ([2023](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.4056622)); Du and co-workers combine Monte Carlo ray tracing, pore-scale simulation and PID control to obtain response time constants of 7.0 s for air against 45.9 s for molten salt, rising by 129% as the mass flow falls threefold ([2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.solener.2024.112984)) — the field predicts the time constant, which is to say it predicts the capacitance, and does not measure it; Baigorri and co-workers use transient simulation in the design of an open volumetric receiver coupled to compressed-air storage, with explicit attention to passive mass-flow regulation under non-uniform flux ([2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/app16136633)). The formulation closest to this manuscript's structural concern is the multi-zone unsteady model of Kumar and co-workers, which resolves exchange between absorbers, casing and return air and validates to ±7% ([2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2022.122747)); its multi-zone rather than single-stream construction is worth noting in §5.2 as an existing step in the direction the manuscript argues for. Across all of these, the transient is a simulated response, not a measured one from which an assembly-scale capacitance is identified.

Two recent papers bear directly on the identifiability argument rather than on the instrumentation, and the first of them is the most consequential find of this search. Song and co-workers resolve the gas–solid interfacial coefficient in porous media at low Reynolds number by measuring the solid and the gas temperature *simultaneously* at each of a series of axial stations — thermocouple pairs at 2.6 cm spacing, 4 s acquisition — and then solving the discretised gas energy equation directly for the one remaining unknown, rather than optimising it inversely against a temperature field ([Song et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2025.127803)). Their term for having both temperatures at the same place is "data integrity," and their whole argument is that this is what makes the coefficient resolvable; the inverse route, they note, is ill-posed and has no analytical solution. That is §5.1's thesis reached independently and from the opposite direction — the manuscript shows what goes wrong when the solid reference is chosen rather than co-located, and Song shows what becomes possible when it is co-located.

Their Table 1 is worth reproducing in §4.2 or §5.1, because it settles a question a referee will certainly raise about the manuscript's Re exponent. It compiles eight porous-media Nusselt correlations at Re ≤ O(10²) with the temperature set each was identified from, and the Reynolds exponents run 0.2, 0.7, 0.857, 0.96, 0.97, 1.35, 1.66 and 1.97. Their own result is Nu = 0.001 Re^1.66 Pr^1/3 with r² = 0.93 over 13 ≤ Re ≤ 133, from 54 tests across two media, three particle sizes, three Darcy fluxes and three heating temperatures. The manuscript's Nu = 3.11×10⁻⁴ Re^1.443 over Re = 23–94 therefore sits inside the range of measured low-Re porous-media exponents rather than outside it, with a prefactor of the same order as the two superlinear entries. The definitions are not interchangeable — Song's is a volumetric interfacial coefficient on particle diameter in a packed bed, the manuscript's an apparent wall-referenced coefficient in a honeycomb — and the comparison should be made as an order-of-magnitude and exponent-range comparison with that difference stated. Made carefully, it converts the manuscript's most exposed number from an anomaly into a member of a family.

The transient technique the manuscript's cooling identification is adjacent to — the single-blow method — is applied to ceramic foam with a sensitivity analysis, but ex situ and between 283 and 323 K ([Xu et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/pr8111490)), which supports the manuscript's position that transient identification is standard in component characterisation and absent from installed receivers. On the closure side, a comparison of three continuum local-thermal-non-equilibrium formulations from the pore to the representative-elementary-volume scale shows that the conduction treatment changes what LTNE predicts ([Kostelecky et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11242-026-02305-5)), which gives the measured nonequilibrium slopes of §4.3 something specific to be checked against.

The transient measurement with an axial profile does exist, and it is in the catalysis literature rather than the solar one: the dynamic operation of monolithic reactors under load fluctuation is studied with the transient as the object of interest ([Pérez-Vilela and Garcia 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhydene.2024.06.148)). This strengthens rather than weakens the manuscript's existing framing in §5.2 that the monolith field is ahead on channel-scale characterisation but works under metered, uniform inflow and a heated wall.

The gap, stated as the search found it: across roughly 850 unique records screened, no experimental campaign published between 2019 and 2026 was found that reports an instrumented internal solid temperature profile at more than one depth through a structured volumetric absorber together with transients from which an assembly-scale thermal capacitance is identified. The absence is worth one sentence in §5.1 and it is worth stating as a bounded search result rather than as a claim about the whole literature.

## Flow maldistribution

The reference §5.2 most needs is [Broeske, Schwarzbözl and Hoffschmidt (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.solener.2022.10.007), which converts the review-level flow-stability criterion the manuscript currently cites through Ávila-Marín's review into a quantitative, absorber-specific methodology. Their two-stage approach uses a one-dimensional LTNE continuum model to identify critical pressure-drop levels with instability potential, then tests those levels in a discrete three-dimensional CFD model against irradiation and flow perturbations; an absorber that cannot compensate a disturbance is judged susceptible to local overheating, and the method is validated against two reference absorbers with known stable and unstable behaviour. Three things make it the right citation. It is honeycomb-specific rather than foam- or mesh-specific. It treats the channel-to-channel flow split as the mechanism of a recognised failure mode, so the manuscript's mechanism is not a novel postulate but a known risk quantified numerically. And its own framing — that "volumetric absorbers carry the inherent risk of flow instabilities" — states the premise §5.2 needs without the manuscript having to argue for it. The companion experimental paper from the same group ([Broeske et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.renene.2023.119220)) then shows what the corresponding measurement reports, which is efficiency and exit temperature, closing the loop with §5.1.

For the constructive half of §5.2 — that the remedy is a multi-stream or distributed formulation with a pressure-coupled flow split, not a better Nusselt correlation — the strongest recent reference is [Nagarajan, Ström and Sjöblom (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-89756-w), who build a reduced-order pseudochannel model precisely because single-channel models cannot predict accurately in realistic geometries where flow maldistribution is present. That is the manuscript's prescription, implemented and published, in the same monolith-channel literature §5.2 already engages with. It is also the natural place to note that the fix is a reduced-order model of a different family rather than an abandonment of reduced-order modelling, which is the concessive point §5.2 already makes.

The Cornejo group, already cited as [17], has extended into flow distribution over exactly the 2023–2026 window: a model for the flow distribution in dual-cell-density monoliths ([Reinao and Cornejo 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/pr11030827)), a predictive model for flow distribution in dual-zone packed beds ([Díaz et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cej.2025.162962)), and pressure-drop models for series-arranged misaligned monoliths ([Cornejo 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ces.2024.120515)) and for dual-cell-density monoliths with experimental validation ([Reinao et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cej.2025.163599)). Citing one or two of these keeps §5.2's engagement with that group current and shows that the channel-scale community treats a non-uniform flow split as the thing to model rather than an anomaly.

Within the solar field, the panel-scale analogue of the manuscript's channel-scale claim was published in 2024. Schwager and co-workers apply a discretised dynamic thermohydraulic model to a commercial-scale molten-salt receiver and show how horizontal flux-density gradients drive the mass-flow distribution among parallel absorber tubes, identifying the combinations of mass flow, flux gradient and mean flux at which the distribution becomes critical ([Schwager et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.52825/solarpaces.v2i.821)). Methodologically closest to the differential-pressure experiment §5.3 proposes is the two-tube fluidized-particle receiver of Sahuquet and co-workers, tested at ambient temperature specifically to isolate the effect of an inhomogeneous flux density on the mass-flow split between tubes ([Sahuquet et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.52825/solarpaces.v1i.717)). Both are receiver-scale, both are 2024, and both establish that flux non-uniformity driving a flow split is an accepted concern in the CSP community — for tubes and particles, which is why the honeycomb case remains open.

The parallel-channel maldistribution physics literature is active but mostly two-phase, and should be cited as analogy with the phase difference stated. Aka and Narayan, working on two thermally isolated, nominally identical, externally heated parallel channels, find flow ratios above ten under some conditions, accompanied by a sharp rise in entropy production that uniform distribution does not predict, which makes the maldistributed state "a thermodynamically favored state over equally distributed flow" ([2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2024.125564)); they extend the result to non-identical channels. Rahman and Weibel are the more important of the two for the manuscript, and they cut against it in a way §5.2 should address rather than cite for support. Channel-to-channel thermal coupling is known to *alleviate* maldistribution, and their contribution is to explain why the benefit saturates: a lumped-capacitance two-phase model shows a single eigenvalue determines whether maldistribution occurs, and above a critical lateral thermal conductance there is no further stabilising benefit ([2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2024.125671)). Since the manuscript's radiative cross-coupling number of 1.50 to 5.66 is precisely a lateral conductance among channels, this receiver carries a stabilising mechanism of the kind that suppresses runaway maldistribution, and the measured rise of transfer units with Reynolds number is therefore a result that survives lateral coupling rather than one that depends on its absence. Both papers are flow boiling, and the phase difference should be stated wherever they are cited. Two single-phase measurements are usable without a phase caveat: the flow distribution of high-pressure CO₂ in heated parallel channels ([Zheng et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.supflu.2022.105569)) and header-induced maldistribution measured with compressed air in compact heat-exchanger headers ([Strobel and Mortean 2022](https://doi.org/10.1115/1.4054917)). If §5.2 or §5.3 wants to name a maldistribution index, the metric comparison of [Dąbrowski (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-38784-5) sets out the velocity-, mass-flow-, pressure- and temperature-based definitions side by side.

For §5.3's first recommendation, the pressure-drop connection to the homogeneous-equivalent modelling literature can be made current through the micro-scaling pressure-drop model with experimental validation for dense wire mesh ([Sanchez-Señoran et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2024.125263)) and the standard-cup CFD study of absorber shape ([Ávila-Marín et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.renene.2022.10.032)), both from the group already cited as [6], [7] and [13].

The second gap, again as a bounded search result: no experimental measurement of the channel-to-channel flow split in an irradiated honeycomb or monolith solar absorber was found in the 2019–2026 window. Broeske's honeycomb instability analysis is numerical; Sahuquet's flow-split measurement is a two-tube particle receiver at ambient temperature; Ma's is a supercritical-CO₂ loop. This makes §5.3's differential-pressure proposal a genuinely open measurement rather than a replication, and saying so raises its value.

## What to change in the manuscript

Eight of the recommended references were obtained as PDFs on 2 September 2026 and their content is verified against the source rather than against an abstract: Broeske 2023, Iding 2024, Sharma and Talukdar 2023, Du 2024, Kumar 2022, Aka and Narayan 2024, Rahman and Weibel 2024, and Song 2026. Two additions follow from reading them that were not visible from metadata. First, [Song et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijheatmasstransfer.2025.127803) should be cited twice: in §4.2 or §5.1 for the exponent-range comparison that makes Re^1.443 unremarkable among measured low-Re porous-media correlations, and in §5.1 as independent confirmation that co-located solid and gas temperatures are what make the interfacial coefficient resolvable. Second, the Broeske sentence about per-absorber mass flow being unmeasurable belongs in §5.2 or §5.3, because it establishes that the flow split is unmeasured in the field's best recent honeycomb campaign rather than merely unreported.

One reference still needs obtaining: [Broeske et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.solener.2022.10.007), the flow-instability paper, is the top §5.2 recommendation and is not among the eight downloaded — everything said about it here rests on the publisher abstract and snippet, not the full text.

Beyond those, four insertions and one qualification. In §5.1, after the sentence noting that most campaigns report outlet air temperature and efficiency, add the 2019–2026 instances ([Patil et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.solener.2020.11.045); [Broeske et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.renene.2023.119220); [Zaversky et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.52825/solarpaces.v1i.713); [D'Souza et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applthermaleng.2025.125634)) so that the claim is anchored in current rather than only in decade-old practice. Also in §5.1, add the qualification that high-resolution dynamic validation now exists on the front surface ([Iding et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.52825/solarpaces.v1i.885)) and that the depth direction remains unresolved, which is where the reference-choice sensitivity lives. In §5.2, add [Broeske et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.solener.2022.10.007) where the viscosity-driven redistribution mechanism is introduced, replacing or supplementing the review-level attribution of the flow-instability criterion, and add [Nagarajan et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-89756-w) where the multi-stream remedy is prescribed. In §5.3, cite [Sahuquet et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.52825/solarpaces.v1i.717) and [Schwager et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.52825/solarpaces.v2i.821) alongside the differential-pressure proposal, and state that no equivalent measurement exists for an irradiated honeycomb.

## Search protocol

Twenty free-text OpenAlex queries covering both themes (2019+ for measurement, 2021+ for maldistribution) returned 646 unique articles; ten Crossref bibliographic queries (2022+ and 2023+) added 496 and then 212 more, which matters because OpenAlex's free-text index covers full text only for open-access works and therefore under-returns paywalled Elsevier titles where most of this literature sits. Forward citations were expanded from twelve seed papers — the manuscript's references [1], [2], [3], [9], [10], [11], [16], [17], [19], Ávila-Marín's 2018 wire-mesh experiments, Becker's 2006 flow-stability paper, and the honeycomb-instability and dual-cell-monolith papers found in the first pass — retaining citers from 2022 onward. Nine narrow gap-filling queries targeted viscosity-driven redistribution in heated channels, peripheral bypass, axial thermocouple instrumentation, on-sun transients, capacitance identification and validation adequacy. Titles and available abstracts were screened against both targets, then 42 finalists were resolved to full bibliographic records; abstracts were verified for 20 of them from Crossref or OpenAlex, and the remainder are closed-access records verified at title and publisher-snippet level only. The `abstract_verified` column of the accompanying table records which is which.
