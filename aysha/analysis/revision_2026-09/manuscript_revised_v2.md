# Transient Thermal Characterization and Dimensionless Correlations for a Structured Silicon Carbide Volumetric Solar Receiver, with Consequences for Reduced-Order Model Validation

A. Melhim, A. G. Konstandopoulos, K. J. Kakosimos

---

## Abstract

A monolithic silicon carbide honeycomb receiver — 100 square channels of 1.5 mm side, 0.40 mm webs, 137 mm long, 0.623 open frontal fraction — was characterized in a high-flux solar simulator at three lamp configurations and air flows of 4.5 to 18.3 sL min⁻¹, giving nominal channel Reynolds numbers of 23 to 94. Fifteen heating and three cooling transients were reduced to dimensionless form without recourse to any thermal model, and the receiver's constants identified from the transient eigenstructure rather than by inverse simulation.

Gas–solid exchange is limited at the assembly scale rather than in the channel. The apparent Nusselt number, referenced to the length-averaged wall temperature, is Nu_app ∝ Re_nom^1.470 ± 0.011 (r² = 0.9994, common slope with a prefactor per configuration, 2.55 to 3.20×10⁻⁴), one to two orders of magnitude below the fully developed laminar square-duct values of 2.98 and 3.61 and below the channel-level values of the monolith literature. The side-wall profile inverts as flow rises, the mid-depth probe overtaking the front face at a threshold effectiveness of ε* = 0.666, 0.655 and 0.642 under the wall-averaging convention adopted throughout. The interior-to-wall temperature deficit grows linearly with Reynolds number, at 8.51×10⁻⁴ per unit Re at 107 mm with flux-ordered offsets. The slow thermal eigenvalue is linear in the gas advective conductance, identifying an effective capacitance of 276 ± 37 J K⁻¹ — six times the bare monolith — and a loss conductance of 0.080 ± 0.023 W K⁻¹, with the fifteen heating transients collapsing to within 14% when rescaled on it.

Two results contradict standard practice in receiver modelling. First, the transfer-unit count increases with flow: obtained by integrating the measured axial wall profile rather than through the isothermal-wall identity, it scales as Re_nom^+0.341 ± 0.041, where a single-stream formulation whose volumetric conductance is any fixed function of axial position requires Re_nom^−1. Thermal development occupies a few percent of the channel, so that requirement follows from a constant Nusselt number and evaluates to −1.00 on the measured properties. The sign is wrong rather than the magnitude; the result survives a uniform ±25 K outlet-probe offset and moves by 0.01 under the plausible range of wall-profile extrapolation. Fitting instead a flow-independent axial conductance profile — shared across all fifteen runs, shared as a Nusselt profile, and fitted separately within each irradiance configuration, with up to seven free non-negative nodes — leaves outlet residuals of 36 to 68 K root-mean-square that are monotone in flow, correlating with ln Re at r = −0.94 to −0.997 and falling 90 to 197 K per e-fold, so none of the tested fixed-conductance families reproduces the data and the failure is systematic rather than random. A model closing the gas–solid coupling with a uniform-temperature or uniform-flux coefficient on the fixed channel geometry therefore cannot reproduce this receiver's flow response at any calibration; doing so requires an explicitly flow-dependent conductance or a flow-distribution degree of freedom, for which viscosity-driven channel redistribution and peripheral bypass are the two candidate mechanisms, indistinguishable from temperature data alone. Second, the same fifteen runs yield an assembly correlation spanning a factor of 29 in prefactor and 1.09 to 1.84 in exponent, an inversion threshold spanning 0.57 to 0.79, and a capacitance spanning 122 to 281 J K⁻¹, according only to which solid thermocouple is nominated as the reference and which probe subset and fit window are used. At the highest flux the outlet gas varies by 26 K across the full flow range while the front face varies by 264 K. A model validated against an outlet gas temperature and one or two solid temperatures is matched to a quantity that is not a property of the receiver, which offers a concrete explanation for this field's long-standing observation that measured absorber performance falls short of prediction while calibrated models appear to fit.

These are installed-receiver response indices, not validated transport coefficients: Reynolds number is nominal, and the apparent coefficients are constructed from an outlet-plenum probe and an axial wall average. We identify the instrumentation that would convert them into physical coefficients, and single out a differential pressure across the monolith faces alone — unresolvable with the installed ±200 mbar transducer against an expected 0.2 to 1.0 mbar — as the most informative addition available.

**Keywords:** volumetric solar receiver; silicon carbide honeycomb; local thermal non-equilibrium; transient identification; model validation; dimensionless correlation

## 1. Introduction

Volumetric receivers distribute concentrated radiation into the depth of a porous or structured absorber so that the solid temperature peak sits inside the material rather than on the irradiated face, which suppresses reradiation from the hot front surface and raises the attainable outlet temperature. The defining signature is the volumetric effect, an interior temperature maximum, and the promise attached to it has been substantial: reviews of central-receiver technology anticipate thermal efficiencies above 75% with air outlet temperatures beyond 1000 °C, with silicon carbide absorbers usable to 1500 °C where metallic meshes are confined to 800–1000 °C [1]. Three decades of prototypes have not delivered on that promise evenly. The metallic wire-mesh TSA absorber reached 85% efficiency at 700 °C outlet under an average flux of 300 kW m⁻² and a 60% air return ratio, and the recrystallized SiC SOLAIR absorber reached 70–75% at 750 °C under 370–520 kW m⁻² [3,6], but the Sulzer 1 module delivered a measured efficiency of 68% against 80% predicted by its design code, and the Catrec 2 prototype was destroyed in an attempt to raise its 460 °C outlet temperature [1,6]. Successive reviews of the field, the most recent of them in 2023 [46], state the position plainly: the theoretical volumetric effect has not been validated by experiment, and most tested absorbers have performed worse than predicted [3].

The response has largely been to build better absorbers. Fend and co-workers measured thermophysical and heat transfer properties of cordierite, SSiC and CBSiC foams, SiC fibre meshes and catalyst carriers, obtaining efficiencies of 75–92% at 300–550 °C for combined material systems and showing that 45 ppi foams transfer far more heat than 10–20 ppi foams by virtue of specific surface areas of up to 2500 m² m⁻³ [4]; they also reported double-layer SiC foams reaching 98% efficiency at 672 °C outlet [5]. Ávila-Marín and colleagues built and tested twenty-six graded-porosity wire-mesh absorbers against the TSA and SOLAIR baselines [6], and later five periodic and non-periodic SiC morphologies including Voronoi structures that outperformed a conventional SiC honeycomb [7]. Zaversky and co-workers optimized single- against multi-layer ceramic foams and concluded that only the first layer's properties matter thermally, with a broad efficiency plateau between 30 and 50 PPI [8]. Wang and co-workers tested four porous SiC absorbers and found that reducing pore diameter from 5.83 to 2.22 mm at fixed porosity raised efficiency by nearly three points [9]. The pattern continues in the most recent work. Patil and co-workers built and tested a 5 kW reticulated-porous-ceramic cavity receiver for process heat above 1000 °C, comparing SiSiC, alumina and ceria structures across mean pore sizes [31], and Broeske and co-workers tested screen-printed ceramic and metallic-foil three-dimensionally shaped absorbers in a sixteen-module matrix at DLR's Synlight facility, reaching air exit temperatures to 735 °C and thermal efficiencies up to 91.8% at 650 °C against 84.6 to 85.3% for the 80 cpsi HiTRec SiSiC honeycomb benchmark [29]. D'Souza and co-workers took the complementary route of a calorimetric test bench, measuring the heat transfer to pressurised gas in absorbers of varying rectangular channel geometry under a high-flux solar simulator [32]. The recurring output of this family of work is a design recommendation — a porosity, a cell density, a layering, a channel aspect ratio — obtained from steady-state efficiency measurements.

In parallel, a modelling literature treats the absorber as a porous continuum with local thermal non-equilibrium and a radiation model, and asks where the volumetric effect should appear. Kribus and co-workers showed with a one-dimensional slab model that an idealized absorber in local thermal equilibrium predicts efficiencies above 95% at 800 kW m⁻² and 1000 °C, which they described as unrealistically optimistic against the roughly 70% that real absorbers achieve, and that combined enhancements might realistically reach 90% [2]. Fend and co-workers modelled an extruded SiC honeycomb of the Jülich Solar Tower design with coupled single-channel and continuum formulations, matching measured outlet temperature and efficiency to within 1–2% once the extinction coefficient of 2300 m⁻¹ had been measured independently [11]. Ávila-Marín, Caliot and colleagues developed a homogeneous equivalent model with a P1 radiation approximation for dense wire meshes, closing the gas–solid coupling with volumetric Nusselt correlations of the form Nu_lv = a Re^b Pr^c [13]. Effective-property work by Mendes and co-workers on CT-reconstructed foams showed that superposing separately computed conduction and radiation contributions does not recover the coupled effective conductivity [14], and Moro Filho and Malalasekera showed that a Rosseland diffusion treatment of the radiation field can differ from a discrete-ordinates solution by up to 76%, failing especially near boundaries [15]. Our own group has contributed to this family, showing by three-dimensional modelling of a honeycomb channel that specular rather than diffuse surface reflectivity produces a more uniform source term and raises modelled efficiency to 96% against 85% for the commercial HiTRec-II geometry [16]. Across all of this work local thermal non-equilibrium is a premise of the equations rather than a measured quantity, and the volumetric heat transfer coefficient that carries the coupling is either taken from a correlation obtained elsewhere or fitted.

The most rigorous heat transfer analysis of honeycomb channels comes from outside the solar field. Cornejo, Nikrityuk and Hayes computed laminar convection in monolith channels under constant wall temperature and constant wall heat flux across Reynolds numbers of 50 to 600, and showed that using an inlet rather than a local temperature to form the Péclet number introduces errors of 50% or more, and that at high heating rates a minimum appears in the Nusselt–Graetz curve some 16% below the asymptotic value [17]. Hayes and co-workers established effective axial and radial conductivities for honeycomb substrates [18], and their thirty-year review of multi-scale monolith modelling notes that radiation is required to reproduce measured axial temperature profiles and — a point of direct relevance here — that a thermocouple reading inside a monolith channel can be significantly affected by radiation even when it does not block the channel [19]. Cui and Kær showed that a wall gap of only 0.05 mm substantially reduces effective radial conductivity in a monolith, with radiation only partly compensating [20], and Schlereth and Hinrichsen showed that a two-dimensional pseudo-continuous reduction reproduces full three-dimensional CFD of a conductive monolith to better than 0.5 K at reference conditions [21]. These results are authoritative, but they are obtained for the channel and the substrate under a heated wall and a uniform inflow — Schlereth and Hinrichsen state the assumption that flow is equally distributed over all channels, and Cui and Kær treat the gas as static — and it is precisely those conditions that an installed receiver under a concentrated, spilling beam does not satisfy.

What no member of either family does is measure an installed receiver through its transients and read the governing quantities directly off the measurement. That gap matters for a specific reason. When a reduced-order receiver model is calibrated, the data available for calibration are almost always a gas outlet temperature together with one or two embedded solid temperatures, because those are the measurements a receiver rig has. Capuano and co-workers give the clearest illustration of what follows: reviewing four established DLR models against one reference experiment, they report predicted outlet air temperatures of 1050, 1048, 1057 and 1073 K against 972 K measured, and efficiencies of 0.78, 0.77, 0.79 and 0.81 against 0.69, and attribute the gap in part to the difficulty of experimentally evaluating a mean air flow temperature [10]. Four independently developed models agreeing with each other to within 25 K and disagreeing with the experiment by 80 to 100 K is not a calibration problem; it is a symptom of a validation target that does not constrain what the models disagree about.

This paper takes the complementary approach. Rather than fitting a simulation and reporting fitted parameters, we reduce the measurements to dimensionless groups, identify the receiver's constants from the transient eigenstructure, and then test the structure of the reduced-order formulations against a measured exponent. A structural test of that kind is immune to the identifiability problems that afflict inverse calibration, because it constrains the sign of a derivative rather than the value of a coefficient. It also lets us do something the existing literature cannot: quantify how much the identified coefficients depend on the analyst's choice of reference sensor, and thereby estimate how much information a conventional validation exercise actually carries.

The contributions are a complete dimensionless characterization of an installed SiC honeycomb receiver, including a directly instrumented rather than assumed wall-to-interior temperature disequilibrium; an identification of the receiver's effective capacitance and loss conductance from transient eigenvalues, with the sensitivity of that identification to sensor selection quantified; a similarity rescaling on which the fifteen heating transients collapse; two estimates of the delivered aperture irradiance, one radiometric and one from an apparent steady energy closure; and two methodological results — that validation against a gas outlet temperature plus one or two solid temperatures is largely uninformative for this class of receiver, and that a uniform-temperature or uniform-flux coefficient evaluated on the fixed channel geometry cannot reproduce its flow response at any calibration.

## 2. Experiment

### 2.1 Receiver and apparatus

The absorber is a monolithic silicon carbide honeycomb with a ten-by-ten array of square channels of 1.5 mm side and 0.40 mm web thickness, giving a pitch of 1.9 mm, a 19.0 mm square frontal face of area 3.61 cm², and an open frontal fraction of 0.6233. The axial length is 137 mm. The measured mass is 40 g, which with the solid cross-section of 1.360×10⁻⁴ m² corresponds to an effective bulk density of 2147 kg m⁻³, consistent with a recrystallized SiC of roughly one third internal porosity; this is the value used for every capacitance estimate below. The geometry is of the same family as the extruded SiC honeycombs and catalyst-carrier substrates characterized in the solar literature [4,11] and modelled in our own earlier work [16], with a channel size between the two samples of Fend and co-workers (2.18 and 1.43 mm) and a comparable web thickness.

The monolith is held in an insulated cavity and irradiated through a circular aperture by the seven-lamp high-flux solar simulator at Texas A&M University at Qatar, each lamp a 6 kW xenon short-arc unit; the apparatus and its optical characterization are described in detail elsewhere [22,23]. Air is metered by four thermal mass-flow controllers, all carrying air, referenced to 21.1 °C and 1 atm, and the total flow is their sum. Because these are mass-flow devices reporting standard volumetric units, the mass flow follows from the reference density, ρ_std = 1.1996 kg m⁻³, rather than from the density at the prevailing ambient temperature; the previous analysis chain used ρ(T_amb), which introduced a 0.7 to 1.9% error into ṁ and hence into Re, Nu and every energy quantity, and that is corrected here.

### 2.2 Instrumentation

Nine thermocouples define the measurement. Three sheathed probes contact the monolith side wall at axial positions of 11, 58 and 107 mm, denoted T8, T12 and T11. Two probes sit in the interior, flow-exposed region at 58 and 107 mm, denoted T9 and T10. One probe, T3, sits in the exit plenum and reports the outlet gas temperature. One further probe, T2, sits in the surrounding insulation and is used in section 4.6 to assess whether the assembly is thermally stationary at the end of a run; the wall-to-insulation drop T12 − T2 is 310 to 770 K, so the insulation resistance dominates radial transfer. The remaining two probes give the cavity ambient, whose mean is used as T_amb. The distinction between the wall chain and the interior pair is not cosmetic: sections 4.3 and 5.1 show that the two report materially different temperatures at the same depth, and that which of them is taken to be the solid temperature changes every identified coefficient in this paper. That the interior probe reading is itself perturbed by the radiation field is established for this class of geometry by Hayes and Cornejo [19], and it is one reason we treat the reference-temperature choice as a systematic rather than a detail.

Differential pressure across the monolith is monitored with a Keller PD33X of ±200 mbar range and ±0.1% full-scale accuracy, hence an accuracy floor of ±0.2 mbar. Section 4.7 shows that this is the same order as the entire expected monolith pressure drop, so the channel is unusable as installed; we report it rather than omit it because the corrective action that follows is concrete and because pressure drop is the quantity that discriminates between the two mechanisms left open by section 5.2.

Thermocouple tolerance is treated per IEC 60584 for type N as the greater of 1.5 K and 0.004|t|, which at the front probe near 1200 K is ±4.4 K rather than the ±2.2 K constant assumed previously. Installation error for the three sheathed wall probes, comprising contact resistance against the monolith and radiative exchange with the cavity, is not quantified, and we declare it as such rather than absorbing it into the stated budget.

### 2.3 Campaign and reduction

Fifteen heating runs span three lamp configurations at nominal aperture irradiances of 456, 304 and 256 kW m⁻².

| Nominal $G_0$ | Runs | Flows |
|---|---|---|
| 456 kW m⁻² | E67–E71 | 7.1 – 15.3 sL min⁻¹ |
| 304 kW m⁻² | E72–E76 | 4.5 – 18.3 sL min⁻¹ |
| 256 kW m⁻² | E77–E81 | 4.5 – 13.8 sL min⁻¹ |

Three cooling transients, C69, C80 and C81 at 10.5, 6.6 and 4.5 sL min⁻¹, follow lamp shutdown with flow maintained. Two additional runs, E82 and E83, repeat E77 and E81; they are retained in the archive for transparency and are excluded from every fit reported here.

Steady-state values are 120 s means at the end of each heating run. The length-averaged wall temperature is the length average of the piecewise-linear profile through the three wall probes with constant extrapolation to the end faces,

$$\bar T_w = 0.2518\,T_8 + 0.3504\,T_{12} + 0.3978\,T_{11},$$

these being the exact trapezoid coefficients for probes at 11, 58 and 107 mm over 137 mm. The previous draft printed 0.248, 0.365 and 0.387, which are the exact coefficients for probes at 11, 57 and 111 mm; the effect on T̄_w is under 1 K, but the claim of exactness was incorrect as printed. Gas enthalpy differences are computed by trapezoidal integration of c_p(T) between T_amb and the relevant gas temperature rather than as c_p(T̄)ΔT.

## 3. Analysis framework

### 3.1 Dimensionless groups

Per-channel mass flow is ṁ_ch = ṁ/100 and the hydraulic diameter is the channel side, D_h = 1.5 mm. Gas properties are evaluated at T̄_g = (T_amb + T_3)/2 except the thermal conductivity in the Nusselt number, which uses the film temperature (T̄_w + T̄_g)/2. The choice of evaluation temperature is not innocuous in this geometry — Cornejo and co-workers show that forming the Péclet number on an inlet rather than a local temperature shifts the apparent Nusselt–Graetz curve by 50% or more [17] — and we therefore state it explicitly and hold it fixed across every variant reported in section 5.1.

The receiver is characterized as a single-stream heat exchanger in the sense of Kays and London [25], with effectiveness and transfer-unit count

$$\varepsilon = \frac{T_3 - T_{\rm amb}}{\bar T_w - T_{\rm amb}}, \qquad NTU = -\ln(1-\varepsilon),$$

from which an apparent assembly-level coefficient and Nusselt number follow as

$$h_{\rm app} = \frac{NTU\,\dot m_{\rm ch} c_p}{P L}, \qquad Nu = \frac{h_{\rm app} D_h}{k(T_{\rm film})}.$$

A notational convention follows from this construction and is used throughout. The Reynolds number is formed on ṁ/100, that is on the assumption that the metered flow divides equally among the hundred channels — the very assumption sections 5.2 and 5.3 argue is violated — so it is a nominal Reynolds number, written Re_nom where the distinction matters and Re where no confusion arises. Likewise NTU_app, h_app and Nu_app are algebraically constructed from the outlet-plenum probe T3 and an axial wall average; none is an independently measured interfacial coefficient. All four are installed-receiver response indices: they are reproducible functions of the metered flow and the lamp setting, and they are the correct objects for the structural arguments made below, but they are not validated transport coefficients and should not be transferred to another geometry as though they were. Extracting physical convective, radiative and conductive coefficients from this dataset is the subject of a companion modelling study and is not attempted here.

Two qualifications are essential and were understated in the previous draft. The second relation is exact only for a stream exchanging with a wall at fixed temperature, whereas here the wall spans up to 321 K between T8 and T11 and its axial profile is non-monotonic in the inverted regime, so the ε–NTU inversion is an approximation rather than an identity, with a bias quantified in section 4.2. And because h_app is defined through NTU, the correlation Nu(Re) is algebraically tied to ε(Re),

$$\frac{d\ln Nu}{d\ln Re} = 1 + \frac{d\ln NTU}{d\ln Re} + \frac{d\ln[c_p/k]}{d\ln Re},$$

so the exponent in a Nu(Re) fit is not independent corroboration of the measured effectiveness range but the same information rewritten. This identity is what makes the structural test of section 5.2 possible, and it also shows that comparing this fitted exponent against channel-correlation values near 0.3 to 0.6 compares unlike objects, because under this definition of h the relevant null is not 0.5 for Nu but −1 for the transfer-unit count. The comparison that is admissible, made in section 4.2, is against other coefficients that were themselves identified from measurement on a porous assembly rather than prescribed from channel geometry.

The apparent wall-to-interior disequilibrium — a bounded surrogate for gas–solid nonequilibrium, for the reasons set out in section 4.3 — is formed from the difference between the wall and the flow-exposed interior probe at each instrumented depth,

$$\Lambda_{58} = \frac{T_{12}-T_9}{T_{12}-T_{\rm amb}}, \qquad \Lambda_{107} = \frac{T_{11}-T_{10}}{T_{11}-T_{\rm amb}},$$

and the volumetric inversion indicator is the front-to-mid wall difference, I = T₁₂ − T₈, which is negative while the front-face probe is the hotter of the two and positive once the mid-depth probe has overtaken it. Four auxiliary groups bound the candidate physics and are used in section 5.2: the web Biot number Bi = h_app t_w/2k_SiC, the Graetz number Gz_L = D_h Re Pr/L, the axial Péclet group Re·Pr·L/D_h, and the radiation–conduction number N_rc = 4σT̄_w³D_h/k(T̄_w).

### 3.2 Transient identification

After lamp shutdown the assembly decays as a single slow mode. For each sensor the late-window excess temperature is regressed as log(T − T_amb) against time and λ is taken as the mean of the per-sensor slopes. Lumping the assembly into one capacitance C_eff that loses heat to ambient through a conductance K_loss while delivering enthalpy to the gas gives

$$\lambda = \frac{\varepsilon \dot m c_p + K_{\rm loss}}{C_{\rm eff}},$$

so that regressing λ against the gas advective conductance x = ε ṁ c_p identifies both constants from slope and intercept with no simulation in the loop. For each cooling transient ε is that of the heating run it decays from, fixed by the experiment log rather than by flow proximity — two heating runs differ by 0.001 sL min⁻¹, so a nearest-flow assignment is not stable. A pooled ε(q) correlation over the fifteen heating runs is also reported, for comparison only. The approach is a transient identification in the spirit of the temperature-wave method Fend and co-workers used to obtain the volumetric heat transfer coefficient of porous absorbers from phase shift and attenuation [4], but applied to the installed assembly rather than to a material sample.

The heating approach to steady state decays with the same eigenvalue in principle, and log(T_ss − T) is regressed over the deficit window 0.07 < u < 0.45, where u = (T_ss − T)/(T_ss − T₀). In practice that identification is conditional in two ways which we report rather than suppress. It depends on sensor selection, because with the lamps on the front probes are held by a local balance between absorbed flux and aperture reradiation with its own short time constant, so their approach is bi-exponential and does not carry the assembly-scale mode: using all six receiver probes gives C_eff = 122 J K⁻¹ whereas using the three deep and outlet probes gives 281 J K⁻¹, and the physical argument for the latter is sound but cannot itself be tested with this sensor set. It also depends on the fit window, because sweeping the deficit window from (0.05, 0.35) to (0.20, 0.70) moves C_eff monotonically from 295 to 200 J K⁻¹ while r² improves from 0.84 to 0.97, so goodness of fit does not select the window. Accordingly we report the cooling identification as primary, since it requires no sensor rule once the source is removed and all six probes share the slow mode; we report the heating identification as a conditional consistency check; and we report a joint fit to all eighteen eigenvalues as the statistically preferred pooled estimate. The disagreement among them is not noise to be averaged away but the subject of section 5.1.

### 3.3 Similarity rescaling

If the lumped description holds, every transient should collapse when time is rescaled on the identified eigenvalue as t* = t/τ with τ = C_eff/(ε ṁ c_p + K_loss). The ε factor belongs in τ because it is the effectiveness that converts the metered flow into the conductance the eigenvalue actually sees. Both the reference constants and the effectiveness are taken from the primary identification and from each run's own measured effectiveness, so the rescaling is consistent with section 3.2 rather than with a pooled correlation. The half-rise value that results is a property of the receiver's response shape and carries no expected value; a single-node lumped system would give ln 2, and the departure from it is itself the measure of how far the receiver is from one state.

### 3.4 Uncertainty

Three kinds of uncertainty are propagated separately and reported separately, because they behave differently and conflating them has misled the interpretation of this dataset before.

Random and calibration error is propagated by Monte Carlo over 4000 realizations, with thermocouple errors systematic within a run and independent between instruments, together with a 0.5 K steady-window drift term and 2% and 1% perturbations on viscosity and conductivity respectively. Reported ± values are Monte Carlo standard deviations and bracketed ranges are 95% percentile intervals. Because the correlation between the four controllers is unspecified, every instrumental term is computed twice, for fully correlated and for independent units. The two agree to within 0.0002 on every exponent and within 0.5 J K⁻¹ on every capacitance, so the assumption turns out not to matter and single values are quoted; both cases are archived. Where a quantity is a fitted slope, its regression standard error is reported alongside and labelled as such, because the two measure different things: the Monte Carlo term is what the instruments contribute and the regression term is the scatter of the runs about the fitted law. For the correlations of section 4.2 the regression term is the larger of the two by an order of magnitude, and it is the one that should be read as the uncertainty of the correlation.

Four elements of that propagation are stated explicitly, because each was implemented incorrectly or without justification in an earlier version and each has been corrected here. First, the type N tolerance of IEC 60584 class 1 — the greater of 1.5 K and 0.004|t| — is a maximum permissible error rather than a standard deviation, so it is interpreted as a 95% bound and halved to obtain the 1σ systematic term; the previous draft applied the same halving without saying so. Second, the 2.5% flow-controller specification is applied multiplicatively, q → q(1 + δ) with δ drawn once per realization, rather than as the fixed 0.1 sL min⁻¹ additive perturbation used previously, which equalled 2.5% only near the bottom of a fourfold flow range and understated the uncertainty everywhere above it. The metered flow is the sum of four Aalborg controllers carrying roughly 25, 33, 28 and 14% of the total, and the 2.5% figure is a per-unit accuracy. It therefore fixes each unit's total variance and cannot be decomposed into two full-variance components. Each unit's error is written as a correlated part of weight √ρ and an independent part of weight √(1−ρ), so the per-unit total is 2.5% for any inter-unit correlation ρ, and four such errors are drawn once per realization and combined with each record's own measured shares. One physical unit error therefore reaches every steady and transient record, with weights differing only because the shares differ. This matters for what reaches an exponent: because the shares vary little between runs, the resulting per-run flow error is almost purely systematic whatever ρ is, so it displaces a correlation's prefactor and barely tilts its exponent. Earlier versions of this analysis drew a fresh aggregate error for each run, which mimicked a run-to-run repeatability the manufacturer does not specify and overstated what reaches the exponents. Third, the class error is a per-sensor calibration offset, so it is drawn once per sensor per realization and shared across all runs, scaled by each run's temperature-dependent tolerance, with the 0.5 K drift term independent per run; a previous version drew it independently for every run, which contradicts calling it systematic and mis-states how it reaches a slope. Fourth, the abscissa of the transient identification, x = ε ṁ c_p, is rebuilt in every realization from that realization's perturbed flow and temperatures and from an ε(q) fit refitted on its perturbed steady data, with the common controller factor shared between the steady frame and the abscissa since it is one physical error; a previous version applied an unjustified blanket 1% multiplier instead.

Systematic bias in the outlet gas probe is propagated as a declared band, because it is not a calibration error and is much larger than one. T3 sits in the exit plenum at a convective–radiative equilibrium between the gas and the surrounding hardware and reads neither bulk gas nor wall temperature; the bias is of order tens of kelvin and is not characterized, a difficulty Capuano and co-workers identify as a principal source of the model–experiment gap in this field [10] and which Hayes and Cornejo demonstrate for thermocouples in monolith channels specifically [19]. Since ε, NTU, h_app, Nu, ε*, η and — through ε in section 3.2 — C_eff and K_loss all derive from T3, each is reported with a δT3 = ±25 K band alongside its Monte Carlo interval. The structural result of section 5.2 is deliberately constructed to survive that band, as section 4.2 shows.

Estimator choice is propagated for the inversion threshold, where it dominates: section 4.1 reports ε* located both by interpolation between the bracketing runs and by a global linear regression, and carries the difference as a systematic.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Steady field, flow response, and the inversion criterion

The steady field against flow at each configuration is shown in Figure 2, and its response is strongly graded in depth. Front-face wall temperature falls steeply with flow, at −33.2, −24.3 and −20.8 K per sL min⁻¹ at 456, 304 and 256 kW m⁻² with r² of at least 0.97; the mid-depth wall falls at half to two-thirds of that rate, the ratios being 0.50, 0.55 and 0.67; and the rear wall barely falls at all, at −1.1, −1.9 and −5.2 K per sL min⁻¹ with r² of 0.07, 0.12 and 0.76. The outlet gas temperature is nearly flow-invariant at the two higher fluxes, at +0.6 and −0.1 K per sL min⁻¹ with r² no greater than 0.03, and falls at the lowest, at −3.1 K per sL min⁻¹ with r² = 0.55. The sign change across flux is a real feature and one that no model in this campaign has yet reproduced.

The quantitative content of that grading is the first component of section 5.1. At 456 kW m⁻² the outlet gas temperature spans 26 K across the entire flow range while the front-face wall spans 264 K, a ratio of ten; at 304 and 256 kW m⁻² the corresponding spans are 51 K against 331 K and 36 K against 194 K.

The side-wall profile inverts as flow increases, the indicator crossing zero within the tested flow range at all three configurations (Figure 4a), and the crossings collapse when expressed in effectiveness rather than flow (Figure 4b). Two things should be said about what the indicator is. It is a difference between two side-wall probes at 11 and 58 mm, so what it detects is a front-to-mid crossing along the instrumented wall chain; it does not locate the three-dimensional solid temperature maximum, which under a Gaussian beam with radial gradients need not lie on the wall at all, and the radial traverse recommended in section 5.3 is what would settle that. And the threshold value is convention-dependent: ε* is formed on the length-averaged wall temperature, and the sensitivity table of section 5.1 shows it moving between 0.57 and 0.79 as the reference probe is changed. What follows is therefore an operational criterion under the stated convention rather than a receiver property, and it is reported as such.

| Nominal $G_0$ [kW m⁻²] | $q^*$ local [sL min⁻¹] | $\varepsilon^*$ local | $q^*$ global | $\varepsilon^*$ global |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 456 | 10.12 | 0.666 ± 0.002 | 11.08 | 0.673 |
| 304 | 8.36 | 0.655 ± 0.002 | 10.32 | 0.673 |
| 256 | 5.08 | 0.642 ± 0.003 | 3.69 | 0.628 |

The two estimators disagree materially. At 256 kW m⁻² the global linear regression places the crossing at 3.69 sL min⁻¹, which is 18% below the lowest run at that flux; only one point in that group is negative and the indicator is strongly concave, so a global fit under-slopes near the bottom and pushes the crossing outside the data. Local bracketing keeps all three crossings inside the measured range, halves the spread in ε* from 0.045 to 0.024, and makes it monotonic in flux. The defensible statement is therefore that inversion occurs at ε* ≈ 0.65 with a weak monotonic increase with flux from 0.642 to 0.666 over a 1.8-fold flux range. We do not claim flux independence, since the 0.024 spread exceeds the 0.002 to 0.003 Monte Carlo intervals, and we do not claim a resolved nine-sigma flux dependence either, since the spread is comparable to the estimator systematic. The criterion is robust in form: inversion is set by how much of the available wall-to-ambient temperature difference the gas has recovered, not by flow rate or flux separately. Under the δT3 = ±25 K band, ε* at 256 kW m⁻² moves from 0.579 to 0.704, so the threshold is conditional on the outlet-probe calibration and is stated as such wherever it is used.

This is the experimental counterpart to a result the modelling literature has approached from the other side. Our own three-dimensional single-channel study found that a receiver could be moved into genuinely volumetric behaviour by making the surface reflectivity specular, thereby flattening the source term, without changing porosity or cell density [16], and the field more generally has sought the volumetric effect through material and geometric design — graded porosity [6], cell-size gradients and Voronoi morphologies [7], multi-layer stacking [8], smaller pores [9]. The present measurement shows that on one plain, ungraded SiC honeycomb the side-wall profile inverts simply by raising the flow past a threshold expressible in a single dimensionless number, so on this receiver the side-wall signature of volumetric behaviour is at least as much an operating condition as a design property. The measured quantity is a difference between two side-wall probes, not the location of the internal temperature maximum, so this is a statement about the signature and not a direct observation of the interior peak.

### 4.2 Assembly-scale exchange, and a structural constraint

The apparent Nusselt number follows a clean power law over the full campaign (Figure 3a),

$$Nu_{\rm app} = (3.10 \pm 0.11)\times10^{-4}\, Re_{\rm nom}^{1.443}, \qquad r^2 = 0.971,\ n = 15,$$

where the standard error of the pooled regression is ±0.069 and the instrumental Monte Carlo term is ±0.003. The regression term exceeds the instrumental one more than twentyfold, so the residual dispersion is physical rather than metrological. It is also structured rather than random, and resolving it improves the correlation substantially. Fitted within each irradiance configuration separately the exponent is 1.440, 1.473 and 1.522 at 256, 304 and 456 kW m⁻², each with r² ≥ 0.9996; the pooled scatter is therefore not run-to-run noise but a flux-ordered offset together with a weak monotonic trend in the exponent itself. A common-slope model with a separate prefactor per configuration gives an exponent of 1.470, regression standard error ±0.011 and instrumental term ±0.003, with r² = 0.9994, and prefactors of 3.20, 2.70 and 2.55 ×10⁻⁴ in the same order. We report the grouped model as the primary correlation and the pooled fit alongside it, since the grouped form is both better determined and the honest representation of a dataset acquired at three discrete irradiance levels. Absolute values run from 0.028 to 0.213. The fully developed laminar square-duct reference depends on the thermal boundary condition, and Shah and London tabulate all three for an aspect ratio of unity: Nu_T = 2.976 for a wall temperature uniform axially and peripherally, Nu_H2 = 3.091 for a wall heat flux uniform both axially and peripherally, and Nu_H1 = 3.608 for a uniform axial heat flux with a peripherally uniform wall temperature, with fRe = 14.227 in every case [24]. We adopt Nu_H2 = 3.091 as the primary reference, since the four walls of a channel in the interior of the array see closely similar radiative and convective conditions and the thin webs make a peripherally uniform flux the better idealization than a peripherally uniform temperature; the previous draft used this value without naming the boundary condition. The measured apparent Nusselt number is a factor of 14.5 to 111 below it, and the choice among the three limits moves that range only to 14.0–107 or 17.0–130, so the deficit is one to two orders of magnitude on any of them. It is comparably far below the channel-level Nusselt numbers of order three to four that Cornejo and co-workers compute for monolith channels under either constant wall temperature or constant wall heat flux [17]. Exchange is limited by something at the scale of the assembly rather than by the channel film. The exponent deserves a word of context, because a Reynolds exponent above unity invites the objection that it cannot be a heat transfer correlation at all. It is in fact typical of interfacial coefficients identified from measurement in porous media at these Reynolds numbers. Song and co-workers compile eight such correlations for Re ≤ O(10²) whose Reynolds exponents run 0.2, 0.7, 0.857, 0.96, 0.97, 1.35, 1.66 and 1.97, and add their own, Nu = 0.001 Re^1.66 Pr^1/3 with r² = 0.93 over 13 ≤ Re ≤ 133 from fifty-four tests [33]. The present 1.443 over Re = 23 to 94 sits inside that range with a prefactor of the same order as the superlinear members of it. The comparison is one of exponent and order of magnitude only, since their coefficient is a volumetric interfacial one referred to particle diameter in a packed bed and ours is an apparent wall-referenced one in a honeycomb, so the comparison is between like objects only in the sense that both are identified from measurement rather than prescribed from geometry, and it is offered for that reason alone: it establishes that a superlinear exponent is a recurring outcome of measuring rather than assuming this coefficient at low Reynolds number, not that our value should agree with theirs. It is worth being explicit that this is not a competing channel correlation: the channel-scale work is not wrong, and its Nusselt numbers are the correct answer to the question it asks. Ours is an assembly-scale quantity that happens to be expressed in the same non-dimensional form, and the gap between the two is the measurement of how little of the structure participates.

The exponent, however, is not the interesting quantity, because the identity of section 3.1 shows it to be ε(Re) rewritten. The interesting quantity is the transfer-unit count itself (Figure 3b),

$$N_{\rm prof} \propto Re_{\rm nom}^{+0.341},\qquad \pm0.004\ \text{(instrumental)},\ \pm0.040\ \text{(regression SE)},\qquad r^2 = 0.845,$$

the profile-corrected count defined below; the isothermal-wall identity gives $NTU_{\rm app} \propto Re_{\rm nom}^{+0.389 \pm 0.045}$ and is retained only for comparison.

Transfer units increase with flow. For a single stream exchanging with a solid whose volumetric conductance is any fixed function of axial position — that is, any h(z) not itself dependent on mass flow — the transfer-unit count is

$$NTU = \frac{1}{\dot m c_p}\int_0^L h(z)\,P\,dz \;\propto\; \dot m^{-1} \;\propto\; Re^{-1}$$

with no adjustable constant. Evaluated on the measured properties that requirement is −0.9996 for a fixed Nusselt number and −1.017 for a conductance independent of temperature as well as flow; we write it as −1.00 below. The profile-corrected exponent differs from it by 1.34 in the power of Re and differs in sign. Under a uniform δT3 = ±25 K offset it moves only from +0.320 to +0.377, remaining positive and more than eight standard errors from zero, let alone from −1, so the result is not an artefact of the outlet-probe calibration. This is the paper's central structural result and it is developed in section 5.2.

The auxiliary groups close off the local explanations. The web Biot number never exceeds 3.4×10⁻⁵, so the solid is isothermal across the web thickness and through-web conduction is not the limiting resistance; not conduction in the web — consistent with the high effective conductivities that Hayes and co-workers report for honeycomb substrates [18] and with SiC being at the conductive end of the substrates Cui and Kær compare [20]. The Graetz number reaches only 0.704, and it is the smallness of this number, not its magnitude, that decides the matter. Its reciprocal is the dimensionless axial coordinate of the thermal-entrance problem evaluated at the channel exit, x* = L/(D_h Pe) = 1/Gz_L, which here runs from 1.42 at the highest flow to 5.71 at the lowest. Developing-flow solutions for the square duct reach their fully developed asymptote by x* of order 10⁻¹ [24], so this channel is thermally fully developed over all but a few percent of its length at every condition tested, and the appropriate local closure is the constant fully developed Nusselt number rather than a developing-flow correlation. The previous draft read the same number the opposite way, as indicating development throughout. This makes the requirement of the following section exact rather than approximate, because a constant Nu gives a conductance independent of flow and therefore NTU ∝ Re⁻¹ precisely. Were the channels instead developing, a developing-flow closure would raise h while scaling it no faster than about Re^0.5, driving NTU toward Re^−0.5 and making the sign discrepancy worse rather than better; neither reading rescues a local closure. The axial Péclet group is at least 1460, so axial conduction in the gas is negligible. And the radiation–conduction number is 1.50 to 5.66, so radiative transport across and along the channel is comparable to or larger than gas conduction, which means the solid redistributes absorbed energy axially by radiation and an imposed uniform flux is not the correct boundary condition either; that radiation must be retained to reproduce measured axial temperature profiles in monolith channels is exactly the conclusion of Hayes and Cornejo's review [19].

### 4.3 Local nonequilibrium

Both nonequilibrium indices rise linearly with Reynolds number within every flux group (Figure 4c) and both are small in magnitude, Λ₅₈ spanning 0.031 to 0.064 and Λ₁₀₇ spanning 0.055 to 0.114. At 107 mm the three per-flux slopes agree to within ±2% while the offsets are ordered by flux.

| Nominal $G_0$ [kW m⁻²] | slope [Re⁻¹] | offset | $r^2$ |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 456 | 8.72×10⁻⁴ | 0.0301 | 0.997 |
| 304 | 8.51×10⁻⁴ | 0.0341 | 0.999 |
| 256 | 8.35×10⁻⁴ | 0.0447 | 1.000 |

The correct reading is therefore Λ₁₀₇ = c(G₀) + (8.51 ± 0.36)×10⁻⁴ Re, with a genuinely flux-invariant slope and a flux-dependent offset that falls as flux rises. That coefficient is the common slope of a model carrying a separate intercept for each configuration, with intercepts of 0.0440, 0.0342 and 0.0313 at 256, 304 and 456 kW m⁻²; it is the estimate consistent with the three within-group slopes above, and it is the one we report. A pooled single-intercept fit, 0.0376 + 8.25×10⁻⁴ Re with r² = 0.897, averages the offsets away, reports a coefficient of determination well below the 0.997 to 1.000 achieved within groups, and inflates the standard error of the slope ninefold; the previous draft quoted that pooled coefficient while describing the grouped structure, and the two are not interchangeable. At 58 mm the same structure holds with roughly a third of the slope, at 1.81, 2.65 and 3.11 ×10⁻⁴ per unit Re with r² of 0.984, 0.999 and 0.992. The previous draft described Λ₅₈ as small and independent of flow; it is small, but it rises by 22%, 55% and 35% across the flow range at the three fluxes with r² between 0.984 and 0.999, which is not scatter, and the corrected statement is the stronger one that nonequilibrium is a smooth monotonic function of both depth and flow rather than a switch.

What these indices are, and are not, requires care. T9 and T10 are flow-exposed sheathed thermocouples, not bulk-gas measurements: each sits at a convective–radiative–conductive equilibrium among the gas, the surrounding solid and its own stem, and Hayes and Cornejo established for exactly this geometry that a probe inside a monolith channel is significantly perturbed by radiation even without blocking the channel [19]. Λ is therefore an apparent wall-to-interior temperature disequilibrium, not a measured gas–solid nonequilibrium, and it is properly read as an observation-weighted surrogate for the latter — most usefully as a bound, since radiative loading of the interior probe biases it toward the solid and so understates the true gas–solid gap. What is nonetheless gained is that the quantity is observed rather than inferred from a closure. Local thermal non-equilibrium is built into the two-equation formulations that dominate porous-absorber modelling [2,11,13,15] and into the heterogeneous monolith models of the catalysis literature [18,21], and in every case its magnitude follows from an assumed or fitted volumetric coefficient; here a directly instrumented disequilibrium at two depths gives the modelling literature something to be checked against, provided the comparison is made against the same observation model rather than against a bulk-gas temperature. That the gap grows with Reynolds number is also the microscopic counterpart of section 4.2: as flow rises the gas falls further behind the solid at every depth, and yet the integrated transfer-unit count rises. Taken at face value, both statements point to a participating gas–solid contact that increases with flow; the conditional is discharged in section 5.2, where the alternatives that would mimic the same signature are set out.

### 4.4 Transient identification and the energy budget

The three cooling transients decay as a single exponential in every one of the six receiver probes, with a per-sensor eigenvalue spread of 3.7% (Figure 5a). Regressing λ against the gas advective conductance (Figure 5b) gives C_eff = 299 ± 41 J K⁻¹ and K_loss = 0.095 ± 0.025 W K⁻¹ with r² = 0.964 when the abscissa is formed on a pooled effectiveness. The paragraph below refines the abscissa and supersedes these values; both rest on three eigenvalues and therefore on one degree of freedom, which is the caveat that matters most here. The joint fit to all eighteen eigenvalues gives 269 ± 30 J K⁻¹ and 0.101 ± 0.022 W K⁻¹ with sixteen degrees of freedom and r² = 0.899, and is the statistically preferred pooled estimate, while the heating-only identification on the deep and outlet probes gives 281 ± 36 J K⁻¹ and 0.114 ± 0.028 W K⁻¹.

The three agree within their intervals, and the spread among them, 269 to 299 J K⁻¹ or 11%, is the honest measure of how well this dataset determines the receiver's capacitance. One further choice belongs in that spread, and on inspection it should govern. The abscissa x = ε ṁ c_p requires an effectiveness for each cooling run, and a pooled linear fit of ε against flow across all fifteen heating runs was used in earlier versions of this analysis and averages over the flux ordering of ε. Substituting each cooling run's own matched preceding heating effectiveness, with c_p evaluated on that cooling run's own tail temperatures, gives **C_eff = 276 ± 37 J K⁻¹ and K_loss = 0.080 ± 0.023 W K⁻¹ with r² = 0.986**, against 0.964 for the pooled form. We adopt this as the primary identification. It is the better-conditioned estimator — each cooling run is referred to the state it actually decays from rather than to a campaign average — and it is 8% lower in capacitance and 16% lower in loss conductance than the pooled figure carried by earlier versions of this work. The Monte Carlo mean of the matched capacitance, 280 J K⁻¹, sits above the point estimate because C_eff is recovered as the reciprocal of a slope fitted to three points and is therefore a nonlinear function of it; we quote the point estimate with the Monte Carlo standard deviation and note the bias rather than averaging it away. We explicitly withdraw the previous draft's claim that the heating and cooling identifications agree to within 1%, which rested on a heating value of 305 J K⁻¹ that the analysis pipeline does not produce; the agreement is 5.7% against the cooling value and 4.7% against the joint fit. Two conditionalities travel with the heating number and are quantified in section 3.2: including the three front probes drives C_eff to 122 J K⁻¹, and sweeping the fit window moves it from 295 to 200 J K⁻¹. Figure 6b displays all of these together.

Whichever estimate is taken, C_eff exceeds the bare monolith capacitance of 42.0 to 46.8 J K⁻¹, obtained from the measured 40 g mass and c_p at 600 to 900 K, by a factor of six to seven. Roughly five sixths of the thermal mass that sets the receiver's transient is therefore not the absorber but its holder and insulation. That has direct consequences for start-up and cycling estimates, and it is invisible to a steady-state characterization — which is one reason the quantity is absent from a literature built on steady efficiency measurements [4,5,6,7,8,9], and why it cannot be recovered from absorber material properties alone. It also qualifies a standing expectation about this receiver class. He, Du and Shen open their review of porous volumetric receivers by noting that such receivers have a quick transient response and small thermal inertia by virtue of the short flow path of the heat transfer fluid [46]. For the absorber itself that is correct, and the 42 to 47 J K⁻¹ measured here is indeed small. For the installed receiver it is not, because five sixths of the responding thermal mass belongs to hardware the absorber's material properties say nothing about — so the expectation holds for the component and fails for the assembly, which is the distinction this identification makes measurable.

Transient behaviour is not itself a neglected subject in this field, but it has been characterized by a receiver time constant read from the outlet air temperature rather than by identification of the underlying capacitance. The same review collects the practice: a step reduction in incident power is imposed and the outlet air temperature is monitored to restabilization, giving time constants of about 90 s for the Sulzer receiver, 70 s for HiTRec II, 365 s for the Sandia foam, 660 s for CeramTec and 600 to 840 s for SOLAIR, with the review observing that no standard for receiver transient performance exists and that studies have adopted the time constant for want of one [46]. A time constant is a ratio, C_eff/(x + K_loss), and measuring it at one flow leaves the numerator and denominator unseparated; the range from 70 to 840 s across nominally similar receivers is what that conflation looks like. The identification used here separates them, by measuring how the eigenvalue moves as the advective conductance is varied, and it is the separation rather than the time scale that makes the six- to sevenfold excess over the absorber attributable to the holder and insulation. Where the transient has been resolved in detail it has been by computation rather than by identification. Sharma and Talukdar obtain solid and fluid temperature fields, outlet temperature and pressure drop through start-up, shut-down, clear-sky and cloud-passage flux histories with a coupled transient model [41], and Du and co-workers combine ray tracing, pore-scale simulation and a PID controller to obtain response time constants of 7.0 s for air against 45.9 s for molten salt, rising by 129% as the mass flow falls threefold [42]. A predicted time constant is a predicted capacitance, and the factor of six to seven found here between the receiver's effective capacitance and its absorber's own is the term such a prediction has no way to include unless the holder and insulation are modelled explicitly.

The loss conductance is bracketed by the two determinations at 0.080 to 0.114 W K⁻¹, the heating tangent value exceeding the matched-effectiveness cooling secant value by a factor of 1.41. That is the direction expected if part of the loss path is radiative, though a factor of 1.21 over the relevant temperature interval is weak evidence for a T³-dominated path and we claim no more than the sign. The previous draft reported a bracket of 0.10 to 0.16 W K⁻¹ and a ratio of 1.7, in which the 0.16 was close to the upper 95% bound of the heating estimate rather than a point estimate, so the bracket mixed a point value at one end with a confidence bound at the other.

### 4.5 Similarity collapse

Rescaling time on τ = C_eff/(ε ṁ c_p + K_loss) collapses all fifteen heating transients (Figure 5c). The length-averaged wall temperature reaches half its total rise at t* = 0.205 with a coefficient of variation of 16.8% across runs spanning a fourfold flow range and a 1.8-fold flux range, and the outlet gas reaches half rise at t* = 0.634 with a coefficient of variation of 7.8%. The collapse confirms that a single slow mode organizes the late heating transient, and the offset between the two half-rise values quantifies the phase lag between solid and gas. It does not establish a one-state response: the wall half-rise at t* = 0.205 sits well below ln 2 while the outlet sits close to it at 0.634, so the wall retains a faster component the lumped model does not represent, consistent with section 3.2's finding that the front probes carry their own short time constant. The defensible statement is that the heating transients share a common flow-dependent time scale, set by the slow eigenvalue, on which their late behaviour collapses — which is what a start-up or control model needs — and not that the receiver is a one-parameter dynamic system. We are not aware of transient dynamics of this class being reported for a volumetric receiver.

### 4.6 Delivered aperture irradiance

The aperture irradiance is determined two ways, and they disagree.

The first determination is radiometric flux mapping, performed with the three-step flux characterization procedure established for this simulator and documented in detail in two earlier papers from this group [22,23]. A 250 × 250 mm² stainless steel Lambertian target plasma-coated with Al₂O₃ is mounted on a three-axis precision slider of 0.08 μm resolution and 1 μm repeatability, and a Gardon-type circular foil radiometer — a Vatell TG1000-0 with a colloidal graphite coating, 1% repeatability, 2 mV (W cm⁻²)⁻¹ sensitivity and ±3% transducer calibration accuracy, calibrated under a high-intensity lamp against a NIST-traceable sensor and corrected by calorimetric calibration for the spectral mismatch between the source and the coating absorptivity — is mounted at the target centre and traversed across the focal region on a graded grid, every 0.25 mm over the first 10 mm, every 0.5 mm over the next 20 mm and every 2 mm thereafter, with 60 s stabilization and thirty 1 Hz readings averaged at each station. In parallel, a monochrome CCD camera images the target at five exposures between 1/500 and 1/3333 s, forty frames averaged per exposure with dark-current subtraction, projective correction against an affixed chessboard reference, and high-dynamic-range recombination. The grayscale-to-flux transfer is then fitted; a power law with a = 1117.5 W m⁻² and exponent b = 0.903 was preferred over a linear form, and Gaussian fits to the resulting profiles validate the focal distribution with normalized RMSE of about 0.07 and 0.12 on the two axes [22]. Integrating the calibrated map over the receiver face gives the nominal values 456, 304 and 256 kW m⁻² that label the three configurations throughout this paper, because they identify the hardware setting.

The second determination is an apparent steady energy closure. It is not model-free, and the previous draft's description of it as such was wrong: it takes K_loss from the lumped transient identification of section 4.4, so it inherits that model's single-node structure, and it omits storage by assuming the assembly is stationary at the end of a heating run. Neither assumption is safe here. The insulation probe T2 rises monotonically as flow falls and run duration lengthens, from 308 K to 356 K across the campaign, and its own decay is far slower than any run window, so the insulation is still charging when the heating runs end; a residual storage term of unknown sign relative to the neglected terms therefore remains in the balance. Nor need a K_loss obtained from a cooling secant equal the steady loss under irradiation, since part of the path is radiative and the insulation temperature differs between the two states. At steady state the delivered power must equal the gas enthalpy rise plus the identified loss, so

$$f = \frac{Q_{\rm gas} + K_{\rm loss}(\bar T_w - T_{\rm amb})}{G_0 A_{\rm frt}}$$

should equal unity if the radiometric flux is correct. Evaluated at both ends of the identified K_loss bracket — the matched-effectiveness cooling secant of 0.080 W K⁻¹ and the heating tangent of 0.114 W K⁻¹ — and averaged within each configuration, it does not.

| Nominal $G_0$ | $f$ | Closure estimate | Span reported |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 456 kW m⁻² | 0.989 – 1.134 | 451 – 517 kW m⁻² | **451 – 517 kW m⁻²** |
| 304 kW m⁻² | 1.149 – 1.324 | 349 – 402 kW m⁻² | **304 – 402 kW m⁻²** |
| 256 kW m⁻² | 0.784 – 0.916 | 201 – 234 kW m⁻² | **201 – 256 kW m⁻²** |

We report the two determinations as a radiometric estimate and an apparent-closure estimate, and every power-normalized quantity as a band spanning them. The span should not be read as a bounding interval on the delivered irradiance: because the closure omits storage and inherits a lumped loss model, and because T3 bias enters Q_gas directly, neither determination is established as an upper or a lower bound, and the disagreement cannot presently be assigned to any one cause. Gauge spatial averaging, quantified immediately below, is one contributor that follows from geometry alone; the remainder awaits a measured stationarity assessment and an independent outlet enthalpy. Apparent efficiency on the nominal basis ranges from 0.252 to 1.224 and exceeds unity in the 304 kW m⁻² group, which is what forces the accounting question rather than resolving it.

Three considerations make a reported span the defensible object here rather than an evasion. The first is a specific and quantifiable limitation of the radiometric determination at this receiver's scale. The Gardon gauge does not make a point measurement; it reports the total incident flux over its exposed area of 12.6 mm radius [22], an area of 499 mm² against the receiver's 361 mm² frontal face, a ratio of 1.38. In a focused, approximately Gaussian spot [22] the gauge therefore averages over a larger and more peripheral region than the receiver intercepts, and under-reads the flux the face actually sees. Evaluating the ratio of the face-averaged to the gauge-averaged flux for a centred Gaussian gives 1.28, 1.17, 1.11 and 1.04 for 1/e² radii of 10, 14, 18 and 30 mm, which brackets the 1.13 the closure requires at 456 kW m⁻² and covers roughly half of the 1.32 it requires at 304 kW m⁻². The geometric bias thus has the right sign and approximately the right magnitude at the two higher fluxes, and it is a structural feature of characterizing a 19 mm receiver with a 25 mm gauge rather than an error of execution. It does not explain the 256 kW m⁻² configuration, where the closure sits 8 to 16% below nominal; a different lamp configuration produces a broader spot for which the geometric factor tends to unity, and spillage past a small aperture then dominates in the opposite direction. To this must be added the ±3% transducer calibration, the calorimetric spectral correction, and the fitted grayscale-to-flux transfer, each contributing at the few-percent level to an integrated power over a small central area. That directly measured incident power on this apparatus is difficult to convert into power actually delivered to a small target is not a new observation for this rig: in a directly irradiated reactor on the same simulator, optical modelling inferred that only 1 to 6% of the power incident on the reactor aperture reached the catalytic bed, and the reported light-to-chemical efficiency moved from below 1% to 5–25% according to which denominator was used [23].

The second consideration is that the closure is not independent of T3, because Q_gas is computed from T3, whereas the radiometric determination is independent of T3. Their difference is therefore bounded jointly by the flux-map error and the outlet-probe bias, and the two cannot be separated by these measurements alone.

The third is that the outlet-probe bias cannot by itself explain the discrepancy. The uniform T3 shift that would bring the closure to f = 1 is −75 K at 456 kW m⁻², −126 K at 304 kW m⁻² and +75 K at 256 kW m⁻². These are far larger than probe physics plausibly supports, they differ in sign between configurations, and within a single configuration the required shift varies by more than 250 K across the flow range. No uniform outlet-probe bias reconciles the two determinations, and no flux-only correction does either, because the required correction is flow-dependent. What the discrepancy therefore requires is a flow-dependent term in the energy budget. It does not by itself identify which one: bypass around the monolith periphery and flow maldistribution among channel groups are two candidates, and both are also consistent with the flow response of section 4.2, but a departure from thermal stationarity or a flow-dependent outlet-probe response would supply a flow-dependent term equally well and is not excluded by these measurements. The convergence with section 5.2 is therefore a consistency, not an independent confirmation.

No temperature-based result in this paper depends on the choice. Effectiveness, transfer units, the Nusselt correlation, the inversion criterion, the nonequilibrium indices, the eigenvalue identification and the similarity collapse are all ratios of measured temperatures and measured flows. Only the efficiency and delivered-power-per-unit-mass figures shift, and those are reported as spans between the two estimates rather than as intervals containing the true value.

Steady-window means of the differential pressure channel compare poorly with the laminar square-duct prediction, computed with a Poiseuille number of 56.91, all metered flow through 100 channels, and properties at T̄_g.

|---|---:|---:|---:|
| E67 | 15.28 | 0.99 | 2.74 |
| E72 | 18.32 | 0.99 | 2.49 |
| E77 | 13.85 | 0.58 | 0.98 |

| E81 | 4.53 | 0.20 | −0.02 |

The measured signal exceeds the monolith-only prediction by 1.4 to 2.8 times at high flow, falls below it at low flow, and goes negative at the lowest flow, which is the signature of a zero offset on a transducer whose ±0.2 mbar accuracy floor — 0.1% of its ±200 mbar span — is the same order as the entire expected monolith pressure drop of 0.20 to 1.0 mbar; the taps evidently span more than the monolith faces. The second differential channel reads a flat −0.26 to −0.30 mbar in every run and appears unconnected. The pressure measurement as installed therefore cannot constrain flow distribution. This is a more consequential gap than it first appears, because pressure drop is the quantity through which the volumetric-receiver literature has historically diagnosed flow behaviour in honeycombs. That diagnosis rests on a criterion with a long lineage. Kribus, Ries and Spirkl showed with a one-dimensional model that absorbers whose pressure loss depends linearly on flow velocity tend inherently towards flow instability [27]; Becker and co-workers established theoretically and numerically that materials with high thermal conductivity and a predominantly quadratic pressure-drop characteristic are the stable ones [28]; and Broeske, Schwarzbözl and Hoffschmidt state the criterion in its operational form, that instability becomes possible when a single pressure-drop level is associated with more than one air outlet temperature, so that a region of the absorber can settle at a temperature different from the receiver's operating point and, if that temperature exceeds the material limit, fail locally [26]. Ávila-Marín's review carries the same criterion into the honeycomb class specifically, identifying a critical flux above which flow through highly porous honeycombs becomes unstable while wire meshes and low-porosity foams remain stable by virtue of their quadratic characteristic [1], and permeability and Forchheimer coefficients obtained from pressure-drop measurements are the standard inputs to homogeneous equivalent models [13]. Two recent monolith measurements indicate where a honeycomb sits on that axis when it is unheated, and how hard the measurement is. Reinao and co-workers report the first experimental validation of a pressure-drop model for dual-cell-density monoliths, using 3D-printed substrates on a purpose-built rig, and find a linear relationship between Reynolds number and pressure drop in every configuration tested, confirming that the drop is dominated by fully developed laminar flow — while noting that the model progressively underestimates the drop as Reynolds number rises, which they attribute to entrance and exit effects becoming significant [45]. Cornejo quantifies that residue for monoliths arranged in series at channel Reynolds numbers of 50 to 320, finding extra pressure drops large enough to require an explicit correction factor, strongly dependent on spacing and increased further by misalignment [44]. An isothermal honeycomb at these Reynolds numbers therefore has a predominantly linear characteristic — the instability-prone side of the criterion — with entrance and exit contributions that are neither negligible nor easily separated from it. Our transducer cannot place this receiver on either side of that criterion, and the reason is exactly the one those two studies identify: what it spans is the monolith in series with its plumbing, so the absorber's own characteristic is confounded with entrance, exit and fitting losses of comparable magnitude. That is the specific loss. We report it because the corrective action is specific and inexpensive, and because section 5.3 shows it to be the measurement that would discriminate between the two candidate mechanisms for everything above.

## 5. Two consequences for reduced-order receiver modelling

### 5.1 Against validating receiver models on a gas outlet temperature and one or two solid temperatures

The standard validation dataset for a volumetric receiver model is an outlet gas temperature together with one or two embedded solid temperatures. Almost every experimental campaign in the literature surveyed above reports precisely this: outlet air temperature and efficiency, with front-face temperature by pyrometer or infrared camera where available [4,5,6,7,8,9], and the convention is explicit at review level, He and co-workers opening their assessment of receiver performance with the statement that the outlet air temperature and the thermal efficiency are the two key indicators by which solar receivers are evaluated [46]. The same review notes that because a non-uniform radiation distribution produces a non-uniform air temperature distribution, more thermocouples are needed than campaigns typically deploy [46] — a call for exactly the instrumentation section 5.3 specifies, made from within the field's own review literature. The modelling studies then calibrate and validate against those same quantities [10,11,12,13]. This is not a historical pattern that recent practice has moved past. Patil and co-workers characterize a reticulated-porous-ceramic cavity receiver by thermal efficiency and air outlet temperature, with thermocouple ports along the lateral steel shell [31]; Broeske and co-workers test a sixteen-module matrix of three-dimensionally shaped absorbers against a HiTRec SiSiC honeycomb benchmark at up to 650 kW m⁻² using thermocouples placed directly behind each module for air exit temperature, an infrared camera for surface emission and a spectrometer for solar-weighted absorptivity [29]; and the multi-zone unsteady receiver model of Kumar and co-workers is validated to within ±7% against quantities of the same kind [34]. The exception is instructive and is discussed below. This campaign contains the same measurements several times over, and it permits a direct test of how much such a dataset constrains. The answer is that it constrains very little, and much less than the apparent quality of the resulting fit suggests.

The gas outlet temperature is nearly blind to the internal field. At 456 kW m⁻² T3 varies by 26 K across the whole flow range while the front-face wall varies by 264 K, so a model reproducing T3 to within its measurement uncertainty across that flux group is unconstrained in front-face solid temperature by an order of magnitude more; at 304 kW m⁻² the ratio is 51 K against 331 K. Worse, T3's flow slope changes sign between configurations, so the qualitative behaviour a model must match is not even consistent across the campaign.

Which solid probe is called the solid temperature then changes every identified coefficient. Recomputing the entire analysis against different solid reference temperatures — the same fifteen runs, the same reduction, the same fitting procedure, changing only which thermocouple defines T_s — gives the following (Figure 6a).

The prefactor spans a factor of 28.9, the exponent spans 1.09 to 1.84, and the inversion threshold spans 0.57 to 0.79. Every row is a defensible choice that a paper could make without comment, and every row is a different receiver. The interior and wall probes at the same depth differ by 21 to 55 K at 107 mm and by 21 to 27 K at 58 mm, and those gaps grow with flow, so no single solid temperature represents the section and the choice is not a detail. It is worth noting that the same sensitivity has been documented on the channel side of this problem from a different direction: Cornejo and co-workers found that forming the Péclet number on an inlet rather than a local temperature moves the apparent Nusselt–Graetz curve by 50% or more [17], and Hayes and Cornejo showed that a thermocouple in a monolith channel is itself perturbed by radiation without blocking the flow [19]. What is new here is the magnitude of the effect at the assembly scale, where it is not tens of percent but a factor of twenty-nine.

The transient identification is equally under-determined. The same eighteen transients yield C_eff of 122, 269, 276 or 281 J K⁻¹ depending on which probe subset and which fit window are used, with the fit window alone spanning 200 to 295 J K⁻¹ and the coefficient of determination increasing as the estimate degrades (Figure 6b). Selecting among these requires a physical argument about which probes carry the assembly-scale mode, and that argument cannot be tested with this sensor set. The factor of 2.3 between the extreme values is not measurement scatter; it is the width of the identifiability basin.

The consequence is that agreement between a receiver model and a gas outlet temperature plus one or two solid temperatures is not evidence that the model has the right internal physics. The coefficient such a comparison identifies is a property of the sensor placement rather than of the receiver, and a model calibrated against it will not transfer to a different geometry, scale or operating envelope. This offers a concrete and rather deflationary explanation for a pattern the field has puzzled over for two decades. Ávila-Marín's reviews record that most tested volumetric absorbers underperformed their predictions and that the theoretical volumetric effect has not been experimentally validated [1,3]; Capuano and co-workers found four established models clustering within 25 K of each other and 80 to 100 K away from the experiment, and named the mean-air-temperature measurement as a principal suspect [10]; Kribus and co-workers found the idealized equilibrium model optimistic by more than twenty efficiency points against practice [2]. If the calibration target does not constrain the internal field, then models can be simultaneously well fitted and structurally wrong, and their disagreement with new experiments is the expected outcome rather than an anomaly.

The exception noted above sharpens rather than weakens the argument. Spatially resolved dynamic validation of an installed receiver does now exist, and its observable is the front surface. Iding and co-workers validate a real-time capable model of the 1080-cup open volumetric receiver at the Jülich solar tower, discretizing each cup into a heating zone and a transport zone, and reproduce the front temperature to a root-mean-square error of 6.78 K and the air temperature directly behind the cup to 7.51 K — an excellent fit, on exactly the two-quantity dataset whose reference sensitivity is tabulated above [30]. They also report that this per-cup performance does not carry to the array, and that simulating the whole receiver requires an advanced optimization of the model parameters. The field can therefore resolve the irradiated surface in space and in time to within a few kelvin and still cannot resolve depth, which is where the identified coefficients are determined; and the gap between a cup that fits and an array that does not is itself a hint of the degree of freedom section 5.2 identifies.

A further external result supports the diagnosis from the opposite direction. Song and co-workers resolve the gas–solid interfacial coefficient in porous media at low Reynolds number by measuring solid and gas temperatures simultaneously at each of a series of axial stations, using thermocouple pairs at 2.6 cm spacing, and then solving the discretized gas energy equation directly for the single remaining unknown rather than optimizing it inversely against a temperature field, which they note is ill-posed and has no analytical solution [33]. Their term for having both temperatures co-located is data integrity, and their argument is that this is what makes the coefficient resolvable at all. The present results are the complement of theirs: what we quantify is the size of the error incurred when the solid reference is chosen instead of co-located.

We therefore recommend that reported receiver-model validations state explicitly which solid temperature defines the reference and, where the instrumentation permits, report the identified coefficient's sensitivity to that choice; where it does not permit, the identified coefficient should be reported as conditional rather than as a receiver property. This is not an argument against reduced-order models. It is an argument that the standard validation dataset cannot discriminate among them, and that the discriminating measurements are different ones.

### 5.2 Against uniform-temperature or uniform-flux heat transfer coefficients in zero- and one-dimensional receiver models

Zero- and one-dimensional receiver models close the gas–solid coupling with a heat transfer coefficient taken from a correlation derived for a duct with a uniform wall temperature or a uniform wall heat flux. This is the standard practice of the field: Capuano and co-workers document one-dimensional receiver models closing the inlet region with a Churchill cross-flow correlation [10], Ávila-Marín, Caliot and colleagues use volumetric correlations of the form Nu_lv = a Re^b Pr^c [13], Moro Filho and Malalasekera adopt the Fu and Kuwahara volumetric forms [15], and the heterogeneous monolith models of the catalysis literature use Graetz-problem Nusselt and Sherwood numbers for a constant wall temperature [21]. The closure is inadmissible for this receiver, and the demonstration requires no model.

Consider the general single-stream form to which every such model reduces,

$$\dot m c_p \frac{dT_g}{dz} = h(z)\,P\,[T_s(z) - T_g(z)].$$

If h is a fixed function of position — which is what a uniform-temperature or uniform-flux Nusselt number evaluated on the local geometry means, since the geometry does not change with flow — then integrating gives N = (1/ṁc_p)∫h P dz, whose flow dependence is that of 1/ṁ alone. The resulting requirement is not exactly −1, because Re is formed on a temperature-dependent viscosity and h on a temperature-dependent conductivity, so we evaluate it from the measured properties rather than assert it: for a fixed Nusselt number the requirement is d ln N/d ln Re = −0.9996 with r² = 0.9999, and for a conductance independent of temperature as well as of flow it is −1.017. The Prandtl number varies only from 0.6834 to 0.6889 across the campaign, which is why the value is so close to −1; we use −1.00 below and the distinction changes nothing quantitative.

The comparison requires one correction before it can be made, because NTU_app = −ln(1−ε) equals that integral only for an isothermal wall, and the wall here spans up to 321 K and changes its axial shape with flow. We therefore also determine the transfer-unit count without that identity, by integrating dT_g/dz = (hP/ṁc_p)(T_w(z) − T_g) through the measured piecewise-linear wall profile with a uniform h, and solving for the value of N = hPL/ṁc_p that reproduces the measured outlet temperature in each run. The profile-corrected counts exceed NTU_app by 1.5% to 8.7%, the correction being largest at low flow where the axial wall gradient is steepest, and the exponent becomes **+0.341**, with an instrumental Monte Carlo uncertainty of ±0.003 and a regression standard error of ±0.040 about the power law; under the same common-slope grouping used for the Nusselt correlation it is +0.356 ± 0.010. The two uncertainties answer different questions and are reported separately throughout: the instrumental term is what the thermocouples and controllers contribute, and the larger regression term is the scatter of fifteen runs about a single power law. The requirement remains −1.00. The sign is wrong.

This inversion assumes a spatially uniform h, so on its own it establishes incompatibility with a uniform, constant-Nusselt closure rather than with every fixed h(z). The broader statement can be tested directly, and we test it in three families. In each, h(z) is piecewise linear and non-negative on evenly spaced nodes, fitted by multi-start least squares in log h, and the question asked is whether one flow-independent profile can reproduce the fifteen measured outlet temperatures. The first family is a single dimensional h(z) shared by all fifteen runs. The second gives each irradiance configuration its own five-node profile, so that a conductance differing with lamp setting or with temperature level is not counted against the hypothesis. The third shares a Nusselt profile rather than a conductance profile, h_i(z) = Nu(z) k_i/D_h with each run's own gas conductivity, which is the direct test of the constant-Nusselt model class.

None of the three succeeds, and the discriminating statistic is not the size of the residual but its ordering in flow. For the shared conductance profile the root-mean-square outlet residual is 64 K with two nodes and saturates at 55, 54 and 54 K with three, five and seven, so the failure is not a want of freedom; the residuals correlate with ln Re at r = −0.95 and fall by 126 to 148 K per e-fold, running from about +80 K at the lowest flow of each configuration to about −70 K at the highest. The shared Nusselt profile behaves the same way, at 56 and 55 K with three and five nodes and −129 to −132 K per e-fold. Allowing a separate profile per configuration lowers the residual in one group and raises it in another — 36, 68 and 52 K at 256, 304 and 456 kW m⁻² — but sharpens the ordering to r = −0.996, −0.982 and −0.997, so within a single lamp configuration, at fixed flux distribution and comparable temperature level, a flow-independent profile fails monotonically in flow. A fixed conductance therefore over-predicts the outlet at low flow and under-predicts it at high flow, in every family and every configuration tested, with root-mean-square residuals of 36 to 68 K and a monotone flow ordering in each case. The declared ±25 K outlet-bias band cannot absorb this, since a uniform offset shifts all residuals together and cannot produce a trend. The best-fitting profiles push their conductance to the rear of the channel, which is where these families best imitate a rising participation, and they still fail.

Two limits of this test should be stated. It assumes the single-stream form itself — one gas stream exchanging with a wall at the reconstructed temperature — so what is excluded is flow-independent conductance within that form, across the node counts and families tested, and not every conceivable reduced-order model. And it inherits the wall reconstruction, whose extrapolations are bounded below but not measured. Neither limit affects the sign or the ordering of the result. All three families, their fitted nodes and their per-run residuals are archived in `results.json` under `fixed_profile_test`.

Two features of the wall reconstruction were checked for their effect on the exponent. The profile is constant-extrapolated over the unmeasured front 11 mm and rear 30 mm, the latter being 22% of the length. Replacing the rear extrapolation with a continuation at half the measured T12-to-T11 gradient moves the exponent from +0.341 to +0.331; a linear back-extrapolation across the front 11 mm moves it by less than 0.003. A full linear continuation of the rear gradient is not admissible: for five of the fifteen runs it places the exit wall below the measured outlet gas temperature, which no single-stream model can produce, so the outlet measurement itself bounds how steeply the rear wall can be falling. The exponent is thus insensitive to the extrapolation at the 0.01 level, against a discrepancy of 1.34. The discrepancy is 1.34 in the power of Re, nearly a full order in Reynolds number across the tested range, and it cannot be absorbed into any coefficient because it is a statement about a derivative rather than a magnitude. Its robustness to outlet-probe error is of a specific and limited kind: displacing T3 by ±25 K in every run moves the exponent only to between +0.32 and +0.38, so the result survives a uniform calibration offset. It is not thereby protected against a flow-dependent bias, which a convective–radiative probe equilibrium will in general produce, and which would act on the slope rather than the intercept. Bounding that is the purpose of the independent outlet-enthalpy measurement recommended in section 5.3, and until it is done the exponent's magnitude carries a systematic uncertainty that the quoted interval does not contain. What a flow-dependent bias would have to do to overturn the conclusion is reverse the sign of a derivative, which requires it to be both large and monotonic in flow.

Nor can it be repaired by a more sophisticated local closure. A developing-flow correlation of the Shah–London or Hausen type [24], of the kind whose behaviour in monolith channels Cornejo and co-workers characterize in detail [17], raises h in the entrance region and scales it as roughly Re^0.33 to Re^0.5, which drives NTU toward Re^−0.5 to Re^−0.67 and moves the prediction further from the measurement. A solid-side resistance cannot be responsible, since Bi ≤ 3.4×10⁻⁵. Axial gas conduction cannot be responsible, since Re·Pr·L/D_h ≥ 1460. Rarefaction and property variation cannot produce a positive exponent of this size at these Reynolds numbers and over this nearly constant Prandtl number range of 0.683 to 0.689. And a uniform imposed flux is not the correct thermal boundary condition either, because N_rc of 1.50 to 5.66 means radiative transport along and across the channel is comparable to or larger than gas conduction, so the solid redistributes absorbed energy axially and the wall condition is set by the coupled radiation field — the same conclusion the monolith-modelling review reaches from measured axial profiles [19], and the reason the porous-media literature finds radiation treatment to matter at the tens-of-percent level [14,15].

If the measured flow dependence is taken at face value, what remains is that the participating gas–solid contact grows with Reynolds number. That conditional is doing work and should be stated: an outlet-probe bias that varied systematically with flow, or a departure from thermal stationarity that varied with run duration and therefore with flow, would produce the same signature without any change in participation, and neither is bounded by this campaign — which is why the two measurements ranked first and second in section 5.3 are aimed precisely at them. Taking the observation as physical, the quantity a single-stream model holds fixed and this receiver need not is the distribution of flow among channels. Two mechanisms in this apparatus produce the required sign, and both have independent support here and in the literature; they are advanced as the leading hypotheses consistent with the observations, not as identified causes. The first is viscosity-driven redistribution among channel groups at common pressure drop: hot core channels have higher viscosity and choke relative to cooler perimeter channels, and as total flow rises the field cools, the viscosity contrast weakens, and the fraction of the array carrying flow — and hence the effective participating area — increases. This predicts transfer units rising with Re, the wall-to-interior deficit rising with Re, and a front-to-mid side-wall crossing, all of which are observed in sections 4.1 to 4.3. It is also precisely the coupling Wang and co-workers identify in porous SiC absorbers, where they report that under strong local thermal non-equilibrium the non-uniform hydrodynamic behaviour is significantly influenced by increased fluid viscosity at the hot spot [9].

The second is peripheral bypass: a leakage path around the absorber through the housing clearance, carrying a share of the metered flow that never contacts the channel walls. Because that path is comparatively cool and its resistance is a weaker function of temperature than the heated channels', its share falls as total flow rises, so the fraction of the metered flow actually passing through the monolith increases with flow — which produces the same sign in the transfer-unit count as redistribution does, and would equally inflate a nominal Reynolds number formed on the full metered flow. The two are not distinguishable from the temperature data alone, and we do not claim to distinguish them here: both are consistent with sections 4.1 to 4.3, and each would require a flow-distribution degree of freedom that a single-stream model lacks. They differ in one respect that matters experimentally, since redistribution among channels conserves the flow path while bypass short-circuits it, and section 5.3 shows this gives them different pressure-drop signatures at the same total flow. Establishing which dominates is the object of the measurement recommended there; the structural conclusion of this section holds under either.

The first of these mechanisms is not a postulate introduced to explain the present measurement. It is the mechanism of the flow-instability criterion that the volumetric-receiver literature has carried for three decades, restated as a continuous rather than a catastrophic effect. The criterion, as section 4.7 sets out, distinguishes absorbers whose pressure loss depends linearly on velocity, which tend inherently towards instability [27], from those with a predominantly quadratic characteristic, which are stable [28], and its operational form is that instability becomes possible when one pressure-drop level admits more than one outlet temperature [26]. The linear regime is the viscosity-dominated one, and the viscosity in question is that of the heated gas; so the same viscosity contrast that can drive a channel group to runaway overheating in the unstable case must, in a stable absorber, still redistribute flow reversibly and to a degree that weakens as total flow rises and the quadratic contribution to the pressure loss grows relative to the linear one. That is the sign this receiver measures. Ávila-Marín's review attaches the criterion specifically to highly porous honeycombs [1], and Broeske and co-workers show for two three-dimensionally shaped honeycomb geometries that geometric features can improve flow stability without automatically preventing instability, concluding that a flow-stability analysis belongs in absorber design alongside efficiency maximization [26]; receiver failures from self-reinforcing local overheating have been observed in absorber tests since the 1990s [26]. Read against that literature, the present result is the quantitative, sub-critical counterpart of a failure mode the field already designs against.

Two results from the parallel-channel literature bear on the mechanism, both for flow boiling, so they are cited for structure and not for quantitative transfer. Rahman and Weibel show that channel-to-channel lateral thermal conductance alleviates maldistribution, that a single eigenvalue determines its onset, and that the stabilizing benefit saturates above a critical conductance [39]; Becker and co-workers likewise found radial heat transfer to reduce the probability of flow instability in porous absorbers [28]. Since the radiation–conduction number of 1.50 to 5.66 measured here is such a lateral conductance, this receiver carries a stabilizing mechanism of that kind, and the measured rise of transfer units with Reynolds number is a result obtained in its presence rather than one requiring its absence. Aka and Narayan add that a maldistributed state can be thermodynamically favoured over an evenly distributed one in heated parallel channels, with flow ratios above ten [40]: a non-uniform split is an available state of such an array, not a symptom of a fault.

That prescription has been carried out elsewhere, which makes the recommendation concrete rather than aspirational. Nagarajan, Ström and Sjöblom build a reduced-order pseudochannel model of a catalytic converter precisely because single-channel models cannot predict accurately under flow maldistribution in realistic geometries [35] — the remedy being a few representative channels carrying different flows rather than one channel with a better closure. The same group whose channel-scale Nusselt work anchors section 4.2 has developed flow-distribution models for dual-cell-density monoliths [36] and dual-zone packed beds [43]. Within the solar field the step has been taken at panel and module scale: Schwager and co-workers resolve the mass-flow distribution among the parallel tubes of a molten-salt receiver panel under flux gradients [37], and the multi-zone unsteady model of Kumar and co-workers resolves exchange between absorbers, casing and return air rather than treating the receiver as one stream [34].

Those two flow-distribution studies do more than set a precedent: they identify what a flow-dependent split requires. Reinao and Cornejo model a dual-cell-density monolith as two concentric regions of differing apparent permeability with a Darcy momentum sink, isothermal, and find the flow fraction through each region depends only on relative permeability and core size, not on flow rate, across several flow rates [36]. That invariance is exact: when every parallel path has a resistance linear in velocity and shares one viscosity, flow rate cancels from the equal-pressure-drop condition. Díaz and co-workers repeat the exercise for a dual-zone packed bed, where the Ergun equation adds an inertial term, and there the split does depend on Reynolds number — strongly sensitive to the resistance contrast at low Re where the viscous term dominates, converging as inertia takes over [43]. A split that changes with flow rate therefore cannot arise in parallel linear-viscous paths of common viscosity; it requires an inertial contribution or a path-to-path viscosity difference. This receiver's participation does change with flow, as Re_nom^+0.341, and both admissible causes are present: the heated absorber carries a non-uniform viscosity field [9], and the viscous-to-inertial transition is the same axis as the linear-versus-quadratic stability criterion of section 4.7 [27,28]. The direction matches too, the packed-bed split growing more uniform as flow rises.

A third agent gives the same structure. In Schwager's molten-salt panels the destabilizing force is buoyancy rather than inertia or viscosity — hotter, less dense salt gains flow in an upward-flowing panel but loses it in a downward-flowing one, where the effect is self-reinforcing and can drive tubes to zero flow — and what arrests it is friction, so reducing overall flow intensifies the maldistribution: below 20% of nominal flow mild flux spreads suffice to trigger reversal, and at 10% no spread is needed [37]. That mechanism is not this receiver's, but in all three cases a driving force for maldistribution competes with a frictional resistance whose relative weight grows with flow, so the split is most differentiated at low flow and most uniform at high flow. The flow-rate dependence of participation is therefore a general property of heated parallel-path arrays rather than an artefact of one geometry.

The scope of this claim should be stated precisely, because it is easy to overstate. We do not claim that no continuum model can reproduce these data, nor an impossibility over all parameter values. We claim the specific and provable thing: a single-stream formulation whose volumetric conductance is a fixed function of axial position cannot produce a transfer-unit count that increases with flow, and this receiver's does. Any zero- or one-dimensional model of that family, however calibrated, will misrepresent the receiver's flow response — and because such models are typically calibrated against exactly the dataset section 5.1 shows to be uninformative, the misrepresentation can survive a validation exercise that appears to succeed.

It is worth adding that the result does not contradict the catalysis literature's successful continuum reductions; it delimits them. Schlereth and Hinrichsen reproduce three-dimensional CFD of a conductive monolith to better than 0.5 K with a two-dimensional pseudo-continuous model, and state as an assumption that flow is equally distributed over all channels [21]; Cui and Kær treat the gas phase as static in obtaining effective radial conductivities [20]. Those are reasonable assumptions for a catalytic reactor with a heated wall and a metered uniform inflow, and the reductions work. What fails here is not the continuum reduction but its use under a concentrated, spilling beam with an insulated housing and a flow split the boundary conditions do not control. Read that way, the present result is a boundary on an established method rather than a failure of it.

### 5.3 What would resolve it

Three measurements would settle the questions left open, and they are worth ranking by value per unit cost. The first and by some distance the most valuable is a differential pressure measurement across the monolith faces alone with a ±2 to ±5 mbar transducer. The two candidate mechanisms of section 5.2 have different pressure-drop signatures at the same total flow, since redistribution among channels conserves the flow path while bypass short-circuits it, so this single measurement discriminates them; the expected signal is 0.20 to 1.0 mbar and an appropriately ranged transducer resolves it to a few percent. It would also connect this receiver to the permeability and Forchheimer characterization on which the homogeneous-equivalent modelling literature depends [13], and it would place the absorber on one side or the other of the linear-versus-quadratic flow-stability criterion [26,27,28] — which, section 4.7 notwithstanding, is a determination this campaign cannot presently make. That such a measurement is practicable at this scale is demonstrated by D'Souza and co-workers, whose calorimetric test bench for pressurised gas receivers carries absolute pressure sensors before and after the absorber alongside the thermocouples [32]. It is also worth noting that no measurement of the channel-to-channel flow split in an irradiated honeycomb absorber appears in the recent literature: Broeske and co-workers state explicitly that individual mass-flow measurements for each of their sixteen absorber modules were impossible for reasons of space, which is why their efficiencies were evaluated through a radiative energy balance rather than through the air enthalpy flow [29]. The nearest published measurements isolate the effect in surrogates — Sahuquet and co-workers vary air velocities in a two-tube fluidized-particle receiver at ambient temperature to impose a controlled thermal heterogeneity and observe the resulting particle mass-flow split between the tubes [38]. The measurement proposed here is therefore not a replication. The second is an independent determination of outlet enthalpy, by mixing calorimeter or shielded aspirated probe, which would break the T3 dependence that currently propagates into effectiveness, transfer units, the Nusselt correlation, the inversion threshold, efficiency and the identified constants alike, and would address directly the mean-air-temperature difficulty that Capuano and co-workers name as a principal source of the field's model–experiment gap [10]. The third is a radial thermocouple traverse at one depth to resolve the core-to-perimeter solid profile, since section 5.1 shows a single solid temperature per depth to be the binding limitation on identifiability; two radial positions at one depth would test the redistribution mechanism directly and would replace the reference-choice sensitivity of Figure 6a with a measured profile.

### 5.4 Limitations

Several limitations bound what has been established. The isothermal-wall ε–NTU relation is an approximation on a wall spanning up to 321 K, and while the bias it introduces is now quantified — the profile-corrected transfer units of section 4.2 exceed the identity values by 1.5% to 8.7%, shifting the exponent from +0.389 to +0.341 — that correction inherits the wall reconstruction, whose front 11 mm and rear 30 mm are extrapolated rather than measured; the rear extrapolation is bounded by the outlet temperature but not determined by it. The wall probes' installation error, comprising contact resistance and cavity radiation, is not quantified. The outlet-probe systematic bias is bounded by declaration rather than by measurement, and only against a uniform offset: a bias varying with flow is unbounded here and would act directly on the exponents that carry the paper's argument. Thermal stationarity at the end of each heating window is assumed rather than demonstrated. The primary transient identification rests on one degree of freedom. The inversion threshold at 256 kW m⁻² is sensitive to the crossing estimator at a level comparable to its flux dependence, and that group contains a single negative point. The flux-map uncertainty is characterized by its discrepancy with the energy closure rather than propagated from the instrument chain. Pressure drop is unusable as installed. The controller-correlation structure is unspecified by the manufacturer, so the instrumental terms are reported as bounds over the two limiting cases rather than as a single propagated value.

Two thermal models are solved in this paper, and the earlier claim that none is used no longer holds: the profile-corrected transfer-unit count and the fixed-conductance falsification of section 4.2 both integrate a single-stream gas energy equation through the measured wall profile. Both are deliberately of the class the paper argues against, and they are used to test that class rather than to correct any measurement. Nothing here resolves what the internal flow field actually is, and no measurement reported in this paper is corrected using a model of it.

## 6. Conclusions

Gas–solid exchange in this structured SiC receiver is limited at the assembly scale rather than in the channel. The apparent Nusselt number referenced to the length-averaged wall temperature follows Nu_app ∝ Re_nom^1.470 ± 0.011 with r² = 0.9994 under a common-slope, per-configuration-prefactor fit over Reynolds numbers of 23 to 94, with prefactors of 2.55 to 3.20×10⁻⁴; the pooled single-prefactor form gives 1.443 ± 0.069 at r² = 0.971. Either way it is a factor of 14.5 to 111 below the fully developed laminar square-duct value Nu_H2 = 3.091 and far below the channel-level values of the monolith heat transfer literature, and a web Biot number of at most 3.4×10⁻⁵ excludes a solid-side resistance as the cause.

The front-to-mid side-wall crossing collapses onto a single dimensionless marker under the adopted wall-averaging convention. The mid-depth side wall overtakes the front face when the gas has recovered about two thirds of the available wall-to-ambient temperature difference, at ε* = 0.666, 0.655 and 0.642 for the three configurations. The criterion is robust in form, while the weak monotonic flux dependence is comparable to the crossing-estimator systematic and is not claimed as resolved. On this plain, ungraded honeycomb the side-wall signature of volumetric behaviour is therefore at least as much an operating condition as a design property, which complements a literature that has sought it through porosity grading, cell-size distribution, layering and surface optics.

The apparent wall-to-interior disequilibrium grows linearly with flow at both instrumented depths, with a flux-invariant slope of (8.51 ± 0.36)×10⁻⁴ per unit Re at 107 mm and a flux-dependent offset, and roughly a third of that slope at 58 mm. Nonequilibrium is a smooth function of depth and flow rather than a threshold phenomenon, and because it is measured rather than assumed it provides a number against which the two-equation modelling literature can be checked.

The receiver's transient is set by six to seven times the absorber's own thermal mass. The slow eigenvalue is linear in the gas advective conductance, identifying C_eff = 276 ± 37 J K⁻¹ from cooling and 269 ± 30 J K⁻¹ from a joint fit to all eighteen eigenvalues against a bare monolith capacitance of 42 to 47 J K⁻¹, with the loss conductance bracketed at 0.080 to 0.114 W K⁻¹. All fifteen heating transients rescaled on this eigenvalue collapse to a wall half-rise of t* = 0.205 within 16.8%, so a single slow time scale organizes their late behaviour. That is what a start-up or control model requires; it is not a one-state response, since the wall retains a faster component the lumped model does not represent.

A transfer-unit count that increases with flow rules out a specific and widely used class of reduced-order model. Determined by integrating the measured axial wall profile, it scales as Re_nom^+0.341 ± 0.041, opposite in sign to the Re_nom^−1.00 that any single-stream formulation with a position-fixed volumetric conductance requires; because the channels are fully developed over at least 96% of their length that requirement follows from a constant Nusselt number, and evaluated on the measured properties it is −0.9996, with a developing-flow closure only widening the gap. Neither a uniform conductance, nor a shared conductance or Nusselt profile of two to seven nodes, nor a separate profile fitted within each irradiance configuration reproduces the outlet-temperature flow response. A zero- or one-dimensional model closing the gas–solid coupling with a uniform-temperature or uniform-flux coefficient evaluated on the fixed channel geometry therefore cannot reproduce this receiver's flow response at any calibration. What is not excluded is a conductance made explicitly flow-dependent: an empirical h ∝ Re^1.34 reproduces the exponent by construction, but it does so without a mechanism and with no predictive content beyond the fitted range. A flow-distribution degree of freedom would supply a mechanism, which is the argument for preferring it — but preferring a hypothesis is not confirming it, and this campaign does not discriminate it from the alternatives above. The successful continuum reductions of the catalytic-monolith literature are not contradicted but delimited: they assume a flow split that a directly irradiated receiver does not control.

Validation against a gas outlet temperature and one or two solid temperatures does not constrain receiver physics. From the same fifteen runs, changing only which solid thermocouple defines the reference moves the identified Nusselt prefactor by a factor of 28.9, the exponent from 1.09 to 1.84 and the inversion threshold from 0.57 to 0.79, while changing the probe subset and fit window moves the identified capacitance from 122 to 281 J K⁻¹; at the highest flux the outlet gas temperature spans 26 K across the full flow range while the front-face solid spans 264 K. Identified coefficients obtained this way are properties of the sensor placement and should be reported as conditional. This offers a concrete explanation for the field's long-standing pattern of models that fit their calibration data while underpredicting new experiments.

Delivered aperture irradiance is not determined to a value, and the two estimates of it do not bound it. Radiometric flux mapping and an apparent steady energy closure disagree by −1 to +13%, +15 to +32% and −22 to −8% at the three configurations depending on which end of the loss-conductance bracket is used, and the discrepancy is flow-dependent, so neither a uniform outlet-probe bias nor a flux-only correction reconciles them. Part of the gap at the two higher fluxes is structural: a Gardon gauge sensing over 499 mm² cannot resolve the flux intercepted by a 361 mm² face in a focused spot, and the geometric bias is 1.04 to 1.28 depending on spot width. We therefore report spans of 451–517, 304–402 and 201–256 kW m⁻². No temperature-based result depends on the choice.

The discriminating measurement is a properly ranged differential pressure across the monolith faces. With an accuracy floor of order ±0.2 mbar against an expected 0.20 to 1.0 mbar signal the installed transducer cannot constrain flow distribution, whereas a ±2 to ±5 mbar device would separate viscosity-driven redistribution from peripheral bypass, which is the open question left by the two methodological conclusions above.

## Data availability and reproducibility

Every derived table, uncertainty estimate and figure input in this paper is produced by one script, `receiver_reduction.py`, from the raw logger files, with no hand-entered constants; the figures themselves are then drawn from those outputs by a second script, `make_figures.py`, which writes Figures 2 to 6. Figure 1 is a schematic of the apparatus and sensor positions, drawn rather than computed, and is the only figure not produced by the pipeline. Quantities that were previously constants in one script and outputs of another, in particular the delivered-power factors, are now computed in one pass, which removes the circular dependency present in the earlier chain.

The script supersedes the previous five-script sequence and corrects three defects in it. The mass-flow conversion used the ambient rather than the controller reference density. Gas enthalpy used c_p(T̄)ΔT rather than the integral of c_p(T). And the committed heating eigenvalues could not be reproduced from the archived code, because the sensor rule documented in that script's own header was not the rule its code applied; the heating identification reported here reproduces the previously committed capacitance to within 0.5% under the documented deep-probe rule, and the six-probe alternative is reported alongside it rather than discarded.

Archived outputs comprise `groups.csv` with the per-run dimensionless groups for the fifteen runs, `groups_replicates.csv` for E82 and E83, `eigenvalues.csv` with the eighteen eigenvalues under both heating sensor rules, `reference_sensitivity.csv` for section 5.1, `pressure_drop.csv` for section 4.7, `flux_geometry.json` for the gauge-averaging calculation of section 4.6, `uncertainty.csv` for the 4000-realization Monte Carlo, and `results.json` containing every reported scalar including the T3 sensitivity band and the heating-window sweep. Following the September 2026 revision, `results.json` additionally carries `ntu_profile`, holding the profile-corrected transfer-unit counts of section 4.2 with their pooled, grouped and T3-band exponents, and `nusselt.grouped` and `nusselt.per_flux`, holding the common-slope and per-configuration correlations; `eigenvalues.csv` carries the ambient and outlet temperatures from which the identification abscissa is rebuilt in each Monte Carlo realization.
