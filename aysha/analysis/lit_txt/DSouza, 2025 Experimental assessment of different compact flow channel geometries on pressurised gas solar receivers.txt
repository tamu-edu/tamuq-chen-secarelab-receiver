ABSTRACT
Pressurised gas receivers using optimised compact flow channels exhibit promising thermal performance in
concentrated solar thermal systems, paving the way to integrate solar energy in high efficiency thermodynamic
power cycles and industrial heat processes. This work is focused on the experimental characterisation of several
compact absorber samples, in order to verify previous numerical analysis aimed at maximising thermal efficiency
while simultaneously minimising pressure drop. Four variants of plain rectangular channel absorber samples
were fabricated varying channel height, breadth and wall thickness. Each absorber sample was experimentally
studied by varying mass flow rate, inlet pressure and incident radiation flux.
The experimental campaign verified important findings and predictions of a previously developed numerical
model including that the maximum thermal efficiency and pressure drop occurs at the smallest channel size
besides the positive effect of taller and narrower channels. The maximum thermal efficiency observed was 95.8%
with the corresponding pressure drop measured at 6.3% of the inlet pressure. This performance, in terms of
thermal efficiency and relative pressure drops, is on par and even surpasses the state-of-the-art receivers of its
type. Such high thermal efficiencies (above 90%) and low relative pressure drops (below 3%) were also observed
for other operating conditions and absorber geometries as well.
1. Introduction
The solar thermal receiver is a critical component of any Concen￾trated Solar Power (CSP) or Concentrated Solar Thermal (CST) system as
it serves to transfer the concentrated solar radiation incident on its
surface as thermal energy to a heat transfer medium. The heat transfer
medium employed in the receiver may be a solid, liquid or gas with this
selection providing a means to classify receivers. Solids, especially as
particles, are capable of attaining high temperatures and use as thermal
storage media but particle conveyance, attrition and transport besides
design difficulties in particle–fluid heat exchangers remain challenges
for solid particle receivers [1,2]. Liquids, especially thermal oil and
molten salt, are widely deployed and are the most prevalent Heat
Transfer Fluid (HTF) in commercial systems [3,4] though the working
temperature bounds of both these fluids create challenges. Thermal oils
cannot operate above 400 ◦C due to decomposition and general chem￾ically instability at higher temperatures [4]. This in turn precludes them
from use in high temperature applications such as next generation
power systems, that typically require temperatures above 700 ◦C [5] or
industrial processes [6]. Molten salts can achieve higher operational
temperatures but have a high freezing point and solidify at around
240 ◦C which leads to salt crystallisation and the formation of plugs that
creates blockages in the circuit [7]. Preventing this requires heat traces
which is a major parasitic energy loss and their implementation incurs a
significant material and financial cost [5]. Additionally, corrosion and
erosion caused by molten salts, especially closer to the receiver when the
molten salt becomes more chemically unstable at higher temperature, is
a concern that needs to be monitored [8,9]. Nanofluids are also
emerging as an active area of research and development in the CST field
[10,11] but their application is limited to lower temperature systems
such as parabolic trough collectors [12,13].
Gas phase receivers can obviate several of these constraints as gases
are able to operate over a broad temperature and pressure ranges
without physical or chemical degradation. Moreover, gases tend to not
be toxic or environmentally hazardous while being relatively inexpen￾sive [14]. On the downside, gases have unfavourable thermo-physical
properties compared to liquids, which leads to poor heat transfer that

In this regard, the design of the flow channels in solar receivers plays
a critical role as they must ensure sufficient heat transfer to the fluid
without it undergoing excessive pressure drop. This is especially true
when the heat transfer fluid is a gas as their compressibility lends them
to additional pressure losses as compared to liquid media. These pres￾sure drops may be alleviated by pressurising the system though this in
turn adds to the system complexity and cost as additional appropriate
pressure-rated components are necessitated [16,17]. The reduction in
pressure drop is primarily due to a reduction in fluid velocity which
occurs at the higher densities, corresponding to an increased pressure
level, for the same mass flow rate [18].
Pressurised gas receivers can be grouped based on the gas employed
(air, helium, nitrogen, etc.), the receiver irradiation pattern (directly,
indirectly or hybrid) and the fluid flow path geometry [19]. The selec￾tion of gas used as the HTF in the receiver is a crucial system design
parameter which may be done based on the application, optimal ther￾mophysical properties of the gas, cost, etc [20]. In the early stages of
pressurised gas receiver development, the primary application of said
receivers was for pre-heating pressurised air before entering the com￾bustion chamber in modified open gas turbine power cycles [21,22].
Given the application had pressurised air as the working fluid in the
power cycle, the simplest system design called for the same pressurised
air to be used as the HTF in the receiver. In other applications, such as
industrial process heat, that usually employ an intermediary heat
exchanger between the solar thermal loop and the process, alternative
gases, with better thermophysical properties than air, may be used to
improve the receiver and overall system efficiency [23,24]. More
recently, the trend toward more thermally efficient supercritical carbon
dioxide (sCO2) power cycles [5,25] has lead to the investigation and
development of pressurised gas receivers using sCO2 as the HTF [26,27].
Classifying receivers based on irradiation pattern has them split into
directly irradiated receivers, which have their flow channels irradiated,
and indirect receivers which have a solid physical separation between
the irradiated surface and the flow channels of the HTF [19]. Directly
irradiated receivers are limited in their operating pressure by the
fragility of the quartz glass window used to allow incident light inside
the pressurised receiver [28].
On the basis of the internal flow channel geometry, pressurised gas
receivers may be classified, non-exclusively, as tubular, embedded
channel, impinging jet and micro/compact channel [19]. Tubular re￾ceivers are by far the most studied and developed [22,29,30], though
alternative concepts exist such as the embedded channel receiver [31],
impinging jet receiver [32–34], Reticulated Porous Ceramic (RPC) lined
cavity receivers [35–37] and compact channel receiver [38,39]. While
tubular receivers boast low pressure drops of below 2 % of their inlet
pressure, the thermal efficiency of such receivers does not exceed 80 %.
Other proposed pressurised receivers have sought to improve on the
performance of the conventional tubular receiver but inevitably face the
same competing considerations of thermal efficiency maximisation and
pressure drop minimisation. While some tubular receiver variants,
notably the flat panel and bladed as well as embedded receivers, are able
to achieve high efficiencies, they do so at the cost of excessive pressure
drops well above 10 % of the inlet pressure. On the other hand, other
receiver concepts, such as the RPC and impinging cavity receivers, have
lower pressure drops below 1 % of their inlet pressure levels. However,
the corresponding thermal efficiencies attained by these receivers is also
lower, often below 60 %. Table 1 provides a summary of select pres￾surised gas receiver designs and performances, as well as the operating
conditions under which they were experimentally characterised.
Compact flow channels offer several benefits such as greater heat
transfer area for the same volume besides improving the heat transfer by
convection due to decreased diffusion length as compared to conven￾tional tubular channels [43]. The reduction in channel size results in a
volume reduction for the same effective heat transfer area, i.e. its
compactness, which in turn causes enhanced heat transfer coefficients

Reviewing the current state of the art in the study of such compact
channel pressurised gas receivers, specifically focussing on experimental
works, a microchannel receiver prototype was developed [38,39] based
on compact heat exchanger concept. It was divided into 12 parallel
channels, each being 1 mm wide and 3 mm high. Rectangular ribs were
attached on top of each channel, to improve the heat transfer to fluid,
having the same width as the channel and a height and pitch of 2 mm
and 1 mm, respectively. The receiver was manufactured using Selective
Laser Melting (SLM) in Inconel 625. The outer (or top) surface of 30 mm
× 30 mm was the irradiated plane. An experimental campaign was
carried out using pressurised air (2–6 bar) as the HTF and thermal ef￾ficiency of around 64 % was observed with a pressure drop of around
750 mbar.
On a smaller scale, absorber panels with a Plated Fin Heat Exchanger
(PFHE) type structure and channel sizes in the order of hundreds of
micrometres were manufactured of Haynes 230 using Electrical
Discharge Machining (EDM) [42,44]. Lab-scale absorber panels of 2 cm
× 2 cm were fabricated and demonstrated to absorb 100 W cm− 2 of
incident flux using supercritical carbon dioxide (sCO2) at 650 ◦C and
80–200 bar as the working fluid, reporting thermal efficiencies around
90 %.
Another receiver prototype, using sCO2 as the HTF and internal flow
channels based on PFHE concepts, was developed by the National
Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). The receiver consisted of a
compact structure with two attached plates having a wavy fin
arrangement between them. The plates, which may be stacked one atop
the other, act as the absorber surfaces of the concentrated solar radiation
and are arranged to form a cavity [41]. To withstand the higher internal
pressures demanded of a sCO2 receiver (up to 25 MPa) while simulta￾neously bettering heat transfer to fluid, a high fin density (up to 32 fins/
cm) and thin walls were designed. The receiver, and its individual
panels, were constructed of Inconel 625 and was predicted to have an
efficiency of around 90 %.
From the review of past studies into pressurised gas receiver design
and development, it is evident that an overarching objective was to
maximise heat transfer to the gas, and ultimately the receiver thermal
efficiency, without suffering excessive pressure drops in the process. To
this end, in a previous work, the authors of this manuscript presented a
numerical analysis into the comparative performances of pressurised gas
receivers with various compact flow channel geometries which
concluded that the Plain Rectangular Fin (PRF) and Perforated Fin (PF)
channel receivers performed best in terms of exergy and energy effi￾ciencies [45]. Furthermore, the influence geometrical parameters,
common to all evaluated receiver flow channel geometry types, was
explored and uncovered optimum geometric configurations that maxi￾mised the exergy and energy efficiency respectively. The four common
geometric parameters selected were the channel breadth, height and
wall thickness besides the number of vertical channels (rows). The
exergy efficiency, which accounts for heat transfer to HTF and its
pressure drop, was largest in receivers having taller and narrower
channels with thicker walls. The energy efficiency, which only depends
on the solar to HTF heat transfer, was maximised in receivers having the
smallest channels with the fewest number of vertical channels. As the
exergy efficiency also factored in the pressure drop, it was found to be a
valuable performance indicator of the receiver as excessive pressure
drops are an inherent, though undesirable, feature of gas receivers. The
parametric analysis concluded that there were optimum geometrical
configurations that maximised the exergy efficiency and an experi￾mental study was proposed to verify these findings.
As a natural continuation of the previous numerical analysis of
pressurised gas receivers, this work presents the experimental evalua￾tion of pressurised gas receivers based on the same compact channel
geometries studied in the numerical models. More specifically, this work
focuses on the experimental investigation into the effects of receiver
flow channel geometry, besides other operational parameters, and its
comparison with the previously developed numerical model. Through
this experimental campaign, this work seeks to verify the predictions of
the pressurised gas receiver numerical model and demonstrate the po￾tential of pressurised gas receivers with optimised compact flow channel
geometries for concentrated solar thermal applications. Attaining high
thermal efficiencies (above 90 %) at low pressure drops (below 5 % of
inlet pressure) is the perennial challenge of solar receiver design, which
most state-of-the-art receivers fall short of as shown in Table 1, and in
this experimental work, it is attempted to achieve these performance
objectives and analyse the factors affecting the proposed receivers’
performances and in so doing contribute to the field of solar receiver
development.
The structure of the manuscript is as follows. The experiment design
and preparation are described in Section 2. Firstly, the experimental
setup itself, including the test facility, its operational boundaries and
instrumentation is presented in Section 2.1 followed by a description of
the absorbers designed and manufactured for experimentation in Sec￾tion 2.2. Multiple absorbers, each with different internal geometries,
were tested in this campaign. The experiment design and overall plan of
experimentation is laid out in Section 2.3. This section details the
operating parameters chosen to be varied and the levels of each
parameter to carry out a characterisation of each fabricated absorber to
evaluate the performance of the absorber at each operating point. Sec￾tion 3 delves into the results of the experiment campaign. The perfor￾mance of each of the absorbers tested is presented and compared against
one another as well as to the numerical model of the pressurised gas
receiver. Finally, Section 4 presents the conclusions of the work as well
as propose future lines of research.

Referring to Fig. 2, when starting from the pressurised air cylinder, the
high-pressure air passes through a pressure regulator, that delivers a
constant output pressure as required by the experiment, before passing
through a mass flowmeter. Compressed air cylinders with a fixed
composition of 79 % Nitrogen (N2) and 21 % Oxygen (O2) were used to
supply the pressurised air throughout the experiment campaign. Abso￾lute pressure sensors are placed before and after the absorber besides
thermocouples to quantify the heat transfer to the HTF as the properties
of the gas vary as a function of its pressure and temperature. This
experiment data was inputted into the fluid property database REFPROP
[50] to obtain the thermophysical properties at the absorber inlet and
outlet with which the performance could be evaluated. A series of
thermocouples are also proposed at various locations on the surface of
the absorber to assess the solid temperature and its variations across the
length and breadth of the absorber. The heated air then passes through a
length of coiled tubing submerged in a cooled water tank before the air is
eventually discharged to the environment at room temperature via a
needle valve to ensure adequate backpressure.
The system instrumentation also includes a charged-coupled device
(CCD) camera and a thermographic or infra-red (IR) camera. Both these
cameras are situated behind the lamps of the KIRAN-42 HFSS and are
pointed towards the testbench. The CCD camera, with a light intensity
filter, is used to safely view the testbench from the front during lamp
operation and is essential in generating the radiation flux profiles/maps.
The thermographic camera allows analysing infrared radiation from the
absorber. A Gardon-type heat flux sensor is utilised to quantify the ra￾diation flux incident on the absorber. Table 2 details some important
instrumentation used in the experimental setup including its operating
range and instrument error. The experimental test setup, along with its
instrumentation, can be observed in Fig. 3. Further information about
the experimental setup can be found in [51] which includes photos and
annotated drawings of the key components, their relative positions on
the testbench and their functions.
Data from the instrumentation (excluding cameras) was communi￾cated through National Instrument data acquisition devices, including C
series modules and chassis, to the LabVIEW software [52]. Through
LabVIEW, experiment monitoring and control was executed and
experiment data was saved at regular time intervals. This data was then
processed in MATLAB [53] to analyse the experimental data and assess
absorber performance.
2.2. Absorber design and fabrication
As the absorbers to be tested were of a relatively small size and were
designed for a one-off experiment campaign, additive manufacturing
was identified as the preferred means of fabrication. Absorbers of
stainless steel (316L) manufactured by Selective Laser Melting (SLM)
will be presented in this work with this material selection guided by ease
of manufacturability and its, relatively, high temperature strength. The
pressurised receiver numerical model indicated that the perforated fin
and plain rectangular fin receivers have the best performances [45].
Given the slight but inescapable inaccuracies of 3D printing holes and
overhangs (sags and shape deformations) [54], it was decided to
manufacture all the absorber samples using the plain rectangular fin
compact flow geometry. Absorbers of different internal flow geometries
were proposed to be built to investigate the effect of the variation of
these geometrical parameters on the performance. The four geometrical

3. Results and discussion

3.3. Comparative performance analysis of absorbers
From Table 6, it is evident that PR1 consistently has the highest
pressure drop which is unsurprising as it has the smallest channels and
hence the largest channel velocity which directly affects the pressure
drop. As PR2 and PR3 have the same wetted area, the measured pressure
drops are similar as the only differentiating influence on their pressure
drop would be the heat conducted through the receiver bulk and hence
transmitted to the fluid, which is a relatively weak effect. PR4 has the
largest number of flow channels and thus, for the same mass flow rate,
has the lowest fluid velocity and pressure drop. Of course, the surface
roughness of each absorber may not be the same and that has an outsized
effect on the pressure drop but this roughness is unknown and is
assumed equal for the sake of this discussion.
It should be noted that most of the differences in measured pressure
drop between absorbers, especially at higher input pressures and lower
mass flow rates, are miniscule and cannot be concluded upon given the
magnitude of measurement uncertainty. The omitted cells in Table 6
correspond to those operating points at which the measured pressure
drop falls within the bounds of experimental error and hence these
values are discarded.
At higher inlet pressures, the pressure drop is expectedly lower as the
channel velocity is correspondingly lower due to the larger fluid density.
The relative pressure drop is always below 1 % at 12 bar inlet pressures
but rises to around 2.5 % when the inlet pressure is 4 bar. At its peak, for
the PR1 absorber operating at 4 bar, peak incident radiation flux of 500
kW/m2 and a mass flow rate of 2 g/s, the pressure drop is 7.5 %.

increased absorptance [63,64]. The increased absorptance causes more
heat to be absorbed from the incident radiation which, in turn, leads to
added heat transfer to the gas. As more radiation power is transferred to
the gas, the energy efficiency of the absorber improves. The higher
temperature of the gas causes also increases its specific volume which
results in a greater fluid velocity and higher pressure drops. This com￾plements the heat transfer as increased channel velocity and turbulence,
due to the greater pressure drop, enhances the convective heat transfer.
As the experiment plan called for experiments at the lower pressure
level (4 bar) to be conducted first, the oxidation on the absorber, and
hence its absorptance, during the 12 bar experiment runs were greater
leading to better energy efficiencies at higher pressures in Table 7 for all
absorbers. The lack of consideration of a varying absorptance makes
energy efficiency a less reliable performance indicator than the thermal
efficiency in this instance. The uncertainties of the energy and thermal
efficiencies were challenging to ascertain as multiple experimental
variables, each with their own uncertainties, interacted and combined
into more complex terms [65]. An error analysis has been performed
conforming to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO)
guide [66] which employs the Taylor Series Method (TSM) to model
experiment errors and their propagation. It revealed that the maximum
and mean absolute error in the energy efficiency was 2.7 % and 2.15 %
respectively and the maximum and mean absolute error in the thermal
efficiency was 3.6 % and 2.8 % respectively. A sensitivity analysis was
performed [51] to gain further insight into the interactions and in￾fluences that shape the efficiencies but it is considered beyond the scope
of this work.
Regarding the thermal efficiency, multiple interesting trends are
observed. It falls with increasing radiation flux which is expected given
the higher thermal losses at the elevated surface temperatures corre￾sponding to higher incident radiation. The mass flow rate has a signif￾icant impact on the thermal efficiency with greater mass flow rates
leading to increased efficiencies due to enhanced turbulence-led heat
transfer and heat evacuation from the absorber surface owing to reduced
thermal resistance in the absorber bulk. The inlet pressure has a less
noticeable effect though it is generally observed that thermal efficiencies
were slightly higher at lower pressure levels which is explained by
increased channel flow velocities, and associated heat transfer co￾efficients, at lower pressures.
The highest thermal efficiency is observed in PR1 at 95.8 % though it
also performs better than the other absorbers at most other operation
points. This improved efficiency is expected given that PR1 has the
smallest channel size and wetted area and thus the largest channel flow
velocity which ultimately results in the best heat transfer to fluid. PR2,
however, follows closely behind PR1 in terms of thermal efficiency
which verifies the findings of the numerical model which found
improved thermal performance with taller and narrower channels with
thicker walls due to improved thermal conduction through the solid
walls. In line with this understanding, PR4 has worse thermal efficiency
than PR2 due to thinner channel walls and the additional horizontal flow
channel. Finally, PR3 exhibits the worst thermal efficiency which was
expected as the broader flow channels increased the thermal resistance
of the absorber which ultimately resulted in higher thermal losses and
worse heat transfer to fluid.
The pressure drop at the highest thermal efficiency (PR1) is 6.3 % of
the inlet pressure and similar values of relative pressure drop are
observed at thermal efficiencies above 90 % at inlet pressures of 4 bar
though these fall to around 0.75 % at inlet pressures of 12 bar. Though
the thermal efficiencies of PR2 are slightly lower than those corre￾sponding to PR1, the pressure drops are also lower with relative pressure
drops around 3 % of the inlet pressure at 4 bar. Again, the relative
pressure drops for PR2 crash to just 0.33 % at inlet pressures of 12 bar.
This not only experimentally demonstrates that the absorbers achieve a
high thermal efficiency without excessive pressure drop but also in￾dicates that, as predicted by the numerical model, an optimum absorber
geometrical configuration has taller and narrower channels.

The experimental setup included a pressurised receiver test facility,
utilising the KIRAN-42 High Flux Solar Simulator as the concentrated
light source. Four stainless steel absorbers, designed with varying rect￾angular channel aspect ratios and wall thicknesses, were manufactured
using Selective Laser Melting (SLM) for this investigation. The experi￾mentation involved characterising each absorber by varying the incident
radiation flux, mass flow rate, and inlet pressure to observe their
performance.
The experimental results indicated that the absorber with the
smallest channels achieved the highest thermal efficiency of 95.8 %,
surpassing several state-of-the-art receivers that typically do not exceed
90 % efficiency. Furthermore, this absorber demonstrated a low pressure
drop of 6.3 % of the inlet pressure, which is superior to similar state-of￾the-art receivers operating at comparable thermal efficiencies. Other
absorbers, which had increased channel height, showed slightly lower
thermal efficiencies, although still above 90 %, and even lower relative
pressure drops of less than 3 %.
The experiment campaign was conducted using pressurised air as the
heat transfer fluid and the thermal properties of the fluid, including its
density, thermal conductivity, specific heat etc, have an impact on the
absorber performance. The results of this study should thus be consid￾ered as specific to pressurised air. Compact channel solar absorbers may
employ a multitude of heat transfer fluids that, for the same flow
channel geometry, may exhibit better or worse performance as has been
observed in this experimental work.
These findings confirmed the numerical model’s predictions,
showing that absorbers with smaller channels provide better heat
transfer to the fluid, and that optimal flow channel geometries might
feature taller and narrower channels. The study also examined the
impact of channel wall thickness, but its influence was less clear
compared to other parameters. A noticeable decline in performance was
observed in the absorber with increased channel breadth, aligning with
the numerical model’s expectations.
One significant source of uncertainty in the experimental results was
the absorptance of the absorbers. The stainless steel absorbers were
untreated and unoxidized, leading to changes in absorptance as the
temperature of the absorber solid increased during the experiments. The
absorptance was measured before and after the experiments using a
spectrophotometer and image processing, which introduced variability
in the results. This issue could have been mitigated by either measuring
the absorptance after each experiment run or by pre-treating the ab￾sorbers to ensure a more consistent absorptance, with the latter being
preferable for simplicity and time efficiency.
Further experimental work can be carried out using the developed
pressurised receiver test facility. Future studies could involve evaluating
absorbers made from different materials or with various surface treat￾ments under different conditions, such as varying mass flow rates, inlet
pressures, working gases, and incident radiation levels. Additionally,
receiver sizes and configurations could also be tested within the facili￾ty’s limits.
