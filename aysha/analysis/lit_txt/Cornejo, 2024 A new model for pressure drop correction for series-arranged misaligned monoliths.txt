A new model for pressure drop correction for series-arranged misaligned
monoliths
Ivan Cornejo
Departamento de Ingenieria Quimica y Ambiental, Universidad Tecnica Federico Santa Maria, Vicuña Mackenna 3939, Santiago, 8940897, Chile
A R T I C L E I N F O A B S T R A C T
Keywords:
Monoliths
Pressure drop
Misalignment
CFD
Channels
This study presents a new model to predict the additional pressure drop for series-arranged misaligned monoliths,
relevant for high-flow industrial applications. Long reactor tubes often contain multiple short monoliths, leading
to extra pressure drops as the flow enters and leaves multiple monoliths. Existing models address aligned
monoliths, but this research considers misalignment, where extra pressure drop is higher. Channel Reynolds from
50 to 320, spacings from 0.1 to 20 channel diameters, and several void fractions are investigated using a discrete
channel model. Results indicate a significant impact of misalignment on extra pressure drops in closely spaced
monoliths due to flow collisions. A correction factor model that adapts to misalignment is proposed, starting
from large values for closely spaced channels and decreasing as spacing increases. The proposed model aligns
with discrete channel data and phenomenological behavior of aligned channels both in trend and magnitude,
showing an R2 above 0.93.
1. Introduction
Monoliths consist of solid pieces with parallel straight channels that
are widely used as catalytic support (Williams, 2001; Lock et al., 2021;
Hayes and Cornejo, 2021). That shape gives high surface area while
maintaining a compact structure, also good structural integrity, low
backpressure and good flow distribution (Önsan and Avci, 2016; Cornejo
and Hayes, 2021; Ibáñez et al., 2021). Initially, monoliths were used
as single substrates in containers (Della Torre et al., 2020; Tischer et
al., 2001; Tronconi and Forzatti, 1992). Later, it became more com￾mon to find applications implying more than one monolith in series
(Chatterjee et al., 2009; Arab et al., 2014; Jang et al., 2018). Monoliths
have gained attention during the last decade because they are partic￾ularly suitable for high-flow applications, such as automotive exhaust
gas cleaning, CO2 hydrogenation to methanol, reforming, and propylene
ammoxidation to acrylonitrile, to name a few (Arab et al., 2014; Azzoni
et al., 2022; Salamon et al., 2020; Tahay et al., 2018; Schädel et al.,
2009). Additive manufacturing has enabled the possibility of proposing
novel monolith shapes and compositions. For example, Quintanilla et al.
(2018) reported a 3D-printed Fe-doped silicon carbide monolithic cata￾lyst for wet peroxide oxidation processes, Busse et al. (2018) compared
open-cell structures made with additive manufacture with monoliths,
and Negri et al. (2018) tested several novel monolith shapes for mono￾propellant thrusters. Our group has also been testing and modeling flow

through monoliths with a non-homogeneous channel size (Cornejo et
al., 2021; Reinao and Cornejo, 2023), while Kane and Northrop (2023)
studied heat transfer in such configurations.
In monoliths, the inner walls of the channels are usually coated with
a catalyst layer a few microns thin called washcoat. Washcoat thickness
prevents internal diffusion limitations, while external mass transfer lim￾itations are usually avoided by selecting small channel hydraulic diame￾ters, typically ranging from 0.5 to 2 mm (Ekström and Andersson, 2002;
Clarkson, 1997). The most typical channel shape is square, however, it is
possible to find monoliths with circular, triangular or hexagonal chan￾nels (Groppi and Tronconi, 1997; Depcik and Hausmann, 2013; Ding
et al., 2023). Commonly, monoliths are made of ceramic materials, but
metallic and silicon carbide substrates have also been tested (Quintero et
al., 2022; Mehdipour et al., 2024). Another option is corrugated metal￾lic monoliths whose channel shape is usually assumed to be triangular
(Avila et al., 2005; Tronconi et al., 2004; Shen et al., 2024). It is worth
mentioning that for all channel shapes, it has been observed that, af￾ter washcoating, the channel tends to become rounder (Watling, 2020;
Peszynski and Tesař, 2020).
Inlet and outlet effects in monoliths, usually taken as minor losses,
become particularly important in high-flow applications. The pressure
drop from these effects scales quadratically with flow rate, unlike other
losses, which scale linearly (Quadri et al., 2010; Carter et al., 1998).
This has also been observed for the so-called wall-flow monoliths used in

In a physical system, experimentally measuring inlet and outlet

the narrow confines of monolith channels. These models are particu￾larly useful for studying channel-scale phenomena, where the physical
insertion of probes can disrupt the very flow fields that are wanted to
measure (Hettel et al., 2013). In the past, CFD has been successfully
utilized to describe channel scale phenomena in monoliths, with many
works reported in the literature for cold and reacting flow (Iwaniszyn
et al., 2021; Canu and Vecchi, 2002; Cornejo et al., 2022; Pawlowski et
al., 2018).

Early models assumed fully developed laminar flow and neglected
any inlet, entrance and outlet effect; hence, the pressure drop through
the monoliths was computed by using the Hagen-Poiseuille equation
(Benjamin et al., 1996). Mixing the latter with Darcy’s law made it
possible to perform reactor scale simulations through the continuum
approach since discrete channel models of a whole reactor are pro￾hibitive for systematic research (Hettel et al., 2019; Bertrand et al.,
2012). Quadri et al. (2010) reported a series of experiments with long
and short monoliths, showing that depending on length, void fraction
and flow rate, the so-called minor losses dominate at a point that the
relationship between pressure drop and flow rate is no longer linear, it
turns quadratic. Among the variables potentially explaining that non￾linearity, upstream turbulence entering the channels was reported to
have no effect on pressure drop (Strom et al., 2011; Cornejo et al.,
2020a; Garretón et al., 2023). Extra losses due to the entrance length
were investigated by Shah and London (1978), and the effect of the
flow entering at an angle into the channels was studied and reported
by Benjamin et al. (2003). More recently, Cornejo et al. (2019) used
a discrete channel model to compare losses at the different parts of a
channel, concluding that, depending on flow rate and void fraction, the
most relevant quadratic losses come from Δ𝑝𝑜, Δ𝑝𝑖 and Δ𝑝𝑒𝑙, with Δ𝑝𝑜
being the highest. Together can represent more than 30% of the total
pressure drop. While such a percentage depends on channel length, this
paper considers the same range of Reynolds numbers and a monolith
with similar void fraction and channel size as in the cited study; hence,
the same trend should also be valid.
Some applications require two or more monoliths in series. Examples
are automotive gas after treatment, with a catalytic oxidation monolith
followed by a selective reduction one, and methanol synthesis carried
out in tube-shell reactors (Arab et al., 2014). The latter is highly exother￾mic; hence, the tubes are filled with catalyst and externally cooled.
Those reactors can have thousands of tubes, several meters long each
(Montebelli et al., 2014). Given the manufacturing limitations of long
monolithic structures, such reactor tubes can contain several monoliths
arranged in series, as shown in Fig. 1(a).

In practical scenarios, achieving perfect alignment of monolith chan￾nels along the entire length of a tube may be uncommon. When misalign￾ment occurs between consecutive monoliths, fluid flow and pressure
drop dynamics exhibit notable differences. As the gap between mis￾aligned monoliths narrows, an increase in pressure drop is observed.
This phenomenon could be explained by the direct collision of the vis￾cous core exiting one channel with the frontal wall or the inter-channel
space of the subsequent monolith. In contrast with the aligned case,
where the correction factor gradually increases from zero, in a mis￾aligned situation, the correction factor should start from a higher initial
value, then progressively diminish towards the asymptotic value of 1 as
the spacing between the monoliths increases.
Currently, to the best of our knowledge, there are no comprehen￾sive data or models available able to accurately capture the dynamics
and quantify the correction factor, 𝑓𝛿 , in scenarios involving misaligned
monolith channels. Focused on that knowledge gap, this paper uses a
discrete channel computational model to investigate the flow field and
pressure drop for misaligned monolith channels. A parametric study en￾compassing a range of channel Reynolds numbers and monolith spacing
lengths is performed. This approach was specifically designed to span
a broad spectrum of 𝛿∗ values, allowing for observing both the initial
and asymptotic values of the correction factor for pressure drop between
substrates in the context of misalignment. The findings are presented in
terms of the flow field and pressure drop, along with their dimensionless
versions. A new model for the correction factor applicable to misaligned
monoliths is proposed. This model, together with existing ones, aims
to provide an accurate computation of the additional pressure drop re￾sulting from the flow exiting and entering consecutive monoliths across
various gap lengths and flow rates for misalignment conditions.
2. Computational model
2.1. Description of the domain
The computational model consists of a representative section of two
identical monoliths with square channels arranged in series, as shown in
Fig. 2(a). The monoliths are misaligned, meaning the center axis of the
first monolith’s channel does not coincide with that of the downstream
monolith. Instead of that, the center axis of a channel of the first mono￾lith lands exactly at the middle of the cross formed for the walls between
channels of the second substrate. Because of that, the first monolith is
directly represented by a channel, while the second monolith is repre￾sented by four fourths of a channel. Fig. 2(b) and (c) show the frontal
and lateral view of such a configuration.
The domain is symmetric; hence, it is possible to reconstruct the
entire substrate by expanding the selected section vertically and hor￾izontally. Exceptions are the channels located at the external boundary
of the substrate that are not perfectly square. Those represent just a
small percentage of the total because a typical monolith has thousands
of channels. Channel 1 had 𝐿1=16.8 mm as length, while channel 2
had 𝐿2=6 mm. These lengths are sufficient to prevent boundary effects
in the results. The channel diameter was 𝐷=1.2 mm for both mono￾liths. The gap between substrates, 𝛿, varies from 0.1𝐷 to 20𝐷, and wall
thickness, 𝑤, moved from 0.16 to 0.80 mm, according to the analyzed
case.
The flow enters the first channel with a prescribed velocity accord￾ing to the analyzed Re, passes to an open section representing the gap
between substrates, and then enters the second channel that ends in a
prescribed pressure outlet.
2.2. Flow model
The investigated channel Reynolds numbers were significantly lower
than the critical one for square channels, so the flow was assumed to be
laminar. Additionally, it was taken into account that literature reports
that when turbulent flow approaches a monolith with channels with sub￾critical Re, the turbulence quickly decays and the flow regime turns to
laminar (Cornejo et al., 2018a, 2017). Careful should be taken because,
according to published literature, turbulence can be generated when
the flow leaves monolith channels (Cornejo et al., 2018b, 2020c; Hettel
et al., 2019). Due to that, Large Eddy Simulation (LES) was used to
corroborate the flow regime assumption. This is described in more detail
in Section 2.4.

As for the boundary conditions, a flat velocity profile was given to
the inlet of Channel 1. The magnitude of the velocity changed according
to the desired channel Re in every case (see Table 1). The inner walls
and rear face of Channel 1 were set as no-slip surfaces. The left, right,
top and bottom boundaries of the gap between channels were considered
symmetry planes. For Channel 2, which is divided into four sections, the
inner walls and frontal face were also set as no-slip boundaries, while the
top, bottom left and right faces represented the center plane of the rest
of the channels, hence, considered symmetry planes. Finally, the four
faces at the end of Channel 2 were outlets with a prescribed pressure of
100 kPa. The operating fluid was atmospheric air at 100 kPa and 20 ◦C
with 𝜌=1.225 kg/m3 and 𝜇=1.7894×10−5 Pa⋅s.
The computational model was implemented using ANSYS Fluent
2023R2 software (2023a), which uses the finite volumes approach
(FVM) (Malalasekera and Versteeg, 2007), in a x2 AMD EPYC ROME
7452 CPU HPC machine, using a total of 64 physical cores. The pres￾sure velocity coupling was solved with the SIMPLE algorithm (Patankar,
1980), and for the momentum term, a second-order upwind scheme was
used (ANSYS Fluent, 2023b). Scaled residuals below 10−6 and a sta￾ble pressure drop were used as stop conditions. The latter was selected
because it is a relevant variable for this study and is sensitive to grid
quality.
2.3. Mesh quality and validation
The domain was discretized into a series of fully orthogonal hex￾ahedral control volumes. The aspect ratio between the edges of the
control volumes was one; hence, the mesh was Cartesian. A view of
the discretized domain can be seen in Fig. 3. The mesh quality was in￾vestigated assuming the most critical conditions, which were a gap size
of 𝛿∕𝐷=20, a wall thickness of 𝑤=0.16 mm and 320 as the channel
Reynolds number. For that case, the results from the two grids were
compared. The first grid, or “normal”, had 1 206 600 control volumes
while the second, or “refined”, had 4 826 400. The refined grid, which
had four times more control volumes, was obtained by decreasing the
element size homogeneously in all directions.
Fig. 4. Validation.
The simulation with the two grids ran until the previously mentioned
stop condition. The pressure drop obtained from the normal and refined
grid was 50.84 Pa and 50.76 Pa. That represents a difference of less than
0.2%, therefore, the study continued with the normal grid.

2.4. Confirming the flow regime
It is known that turbulence may arise when flow exits the first chan￾nel or even when it collides with the walls of the second channel down￾stream (Cornejo et al., 2018c). Such a phenomenon has been studied in
single channels or for perfectly aligned channels but has not been in￾vestigated in a configuration like the one in this paper. For that reason,
in addition to the performed in Section 2.3, a corroboration of the flow
regime was also carried out. The boundary conditions were the same
as in the validation section. LES was used as a flow model since it is
suitable for predicting transitions in the flow regime. The simulation
conditions were the same as those for the validation of the results. That
is, Re=320, 𝛿∕𝐷=20 and 𝑤=0.16 mm. There was a difference in the
boundary conditions of the gap section and Channel 2. Instead of us￾ing symmetry, the outer boundaries were set as periodic, mirroring the
top-bottom and left-right faces in order to capture flow unsteadiness.

CFL number below unity, leading to 𝑑𝑡 ≈ 10 μs. The schemes used were
bounded central differencing for momentum and second-order implicit
for time. Both as implemented in ANSYS Fluent 2023R2 (2023b). The
problem was initialized with the inlet conditions and the stop criterion
for every time step was having scaled residuals smaller than 10−4. The
simulation ran until it converged to a steady solution, corroborating the
laminar steady flow regime assumption. The results from LES agreed
with those from the steady laminar simulation in terms of pressure drop
through the domain and asymptotic shape factor (𝐶) in Channel 1.
Dimensionless static pressure along two lines of the domain obtained
from LES and laminar models are compared in Fig. 5. The figure shows
overlapped curves for both flow models. It is also seen, as expected, that
pressure in line 1 decreases non-linearly in the first part of Channel 1,
and then the curve becomes linear as the flow develops. Entering the gap
between substrates, the flow area suddenly expands. Consequently, ve￾locity progressively decreases, and static pressure increases. Then, line
1 reaches the frontal face of Channel 2 and disappears. The flow collides
that face with the respective increasing of the static pressure. Regarding
line 2, it corresponds to the centerline of Channel 2, hence, it appears
right at the beginning of the gap. It coincides with line 1 in the gap
zone because there is no radial variation of static pressure in that part
of the domain. Opposite to line 1, line 2 does not collide with walls. It
enters Channel 2, where there is a marked reduction of the flow area,
increasing flow velocity and suddenly decreasing pressure.
Results from both channels agree and are consistent with theoretical
expectations in terms of the absolute value of the shape factor in the
developed zone and the trend in static pressure. They serve as evidence
confirming the choice of a laminar flow regime.

4. Conclusions
This study successfully introduces a new model for predicting ad￾ditional pressure drops in misaligned monolith catalyst substrates ar￾ranged in series. The findings highlight a notable impact of misalign￾ment on pressure drops, particularly in closely spaced monoliths. How￾ever, it is important to note that the model is currently limited to mono￾liths misaligned by half of a channel diameter. Additionally, no rotation
of the monoliths is considered. Addressing such limitations would re￾quire a significantly longer set of simulations, which is beyond the scope
of the present exploratory study.
It is concluded that for the studied misalignment, the extra pressure
drop and correction factor differ significantly from that for the aligned
scenario. These factors are significantly influenced by the void fraction,
Reynolds number, and gap length. Higher Reynolds numbers and lower
void fractions result in increased pressure drops due to greater dynamic
pressure and frontal collision. Shorter gap lengths lead to higher pres￾sure drops due to increased interaction effects between fluid and solid.
Dimensionless variables are introduced to generalize the phe￾nomenon in a typical range of conditions for monolith applications.
Based on them, a unified correction factor model, applicable to both
aligned and misaligned channels, extends existing frameworks. By in￾corporating a misalignment modifier, the model predicts the correction
factor across different gap lengths, flow rates and void fractions, enhanc￾ing its practical applicability. Minor discrepancies, such as the slight
curvature in the correlation plot, are acknowledged but acceptable for
practical applications.
Experimental validation of the proposed model is highly recom￾mended, along with an exploration of the effect on the pressure drop
for different channel shapes.
This study also acknowledges that misalignment affects the pressure
drop in the downstream channels. It is expected that non-homogeneous
inflow profiles due to misalignment cause variations in local friction
factor and pressure drop. However, further investigation with longer
channels is needed to fully understand this effect.
