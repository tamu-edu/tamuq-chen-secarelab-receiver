A predictive model for flow distribution in dual-zone packed bed reactors

A B S T R A C T
This paper proposes a new predictive model for flow distribution in dual-zone packed bed reactors, which
consist of two concentric regions, an inner core and an outer ring, each containing pellets with the same shape
but different sizes. This configuration aims to manipulate flow paths for further improving reactor performance.
The proposed model, derived from the Ergun equation, captures how the combination of different pellet
diameters, relative core sizes and inlet conditions influence the flow distribution between the two regions of
the bed. The model’s predictions are compared against reactor-scale CFD data across a wide range of Reynolds
numbers and bed geometry combinations, showing strong agreement for all the tested conditions. According
to the results, different terms of the Ergun equation dominate in different regions of the bed, influencing
the local flow distribution and pressure drop. This insight highlights the importance of considering these
varying effects when optimizing the packed bed reactor design. This study provides a theoretical framework
to better understand and control flow distribution in dual-zone packed beds, with potential applications in
catalytic chemical processes. The proposed model offers a useful engineering tool for novel packed reactor
configurations.
1. Introduction
In the design of catalytic packed bed reactors, controlling flow dis￾tribution is essential for enhancing performance and preventing catalyst
degradation, particularly in exothermic systems prone to hot spots. Hot
spots are especially critical, for example, in emerging renewable synthe￾sis processes, such as the conversion of green hydrogen and captured
CO2
into methanol [1,2], the synthesis of dimethyl ether (DME) [2],
biomass pyrolysis [3,4], and other value-added chemical syntheses.
Flow maldistribution leads to localized regions where reactants convert
at higher rates, creating zones of elevated temperature. This issue is
further exacerbated by interphase resistances between the fluid and
solid phases, causing maldistribution even in uniformly packed beds.
The result is a rapid increase in temperature, particularly in adiabatic
reactors with limited heat dissipation [5,6]. In some cases, maldistri￾bution causes regions of low flow, where reactants accumulate and
undergo faster reactions, generating excessive heat. As these reactants
leave the low flow regions, they mix with cooler components from
other parts of the bed, leading to temperature drops. Such uncontrolled
variations can result from physical obstructions or irregularities in
catalyst packing such as fines posing significant challenges to reactor
stability [5,6].
Achieving a uniform temperature profile in catalytic beds is essen￾tial not only for maximizing reaction efficiency but also for prolonging

catalyst lifespan. Hot spots within the reactor can deactivate catalysts,
reduce selectivity, and promote by-product formation, collectively di￾minishing process efficiency [7–9]. Current strategies to mitigate these
issues include diluting the reactant concentration, lowering the inlet
temperature, using smaller catalyst particles, and employing multiple
adiabatic beds with inter-stage cooling. However, these approaches
often come at the cost of reduced productivity, higher pressure drops,
or increased capital and operating expenses [10,11]. For instance, while
reducing catalyst particle size can mitigate hot spots, it significantly
increases the pressure drop across the bed, raising operational costs.
Similarly, increasing the bed’s thermal conductivity by mixing catalysts
with inert materials helps diffuse temperature gradients but also leads
to higher pressure drops, complicating operations [10,11]. Although
these solutions effectively stabilize reactor performance, they highlight
the inherent trade-offs between safe reactor operation and optimal
efficiency. As a result, achieving better flow distribution without com￾promising productivity remains a core challenge in packed bed reactor
design.
As alternatives to traditional packed bed reactors, various support
configurations have been explored. One promising approach involves
combining pellets with open-cell foams, particularly for non-adiabatic
catalytic processes that require high heat transfer rates [12–14]. Pack￾ing pellets within open-cell foams enhances thermal conductivity while

smaller pellets increase porosity and promote local channeling. No￾tably, despite the foam structure, the pressure drop in packed foam

foams in catalytic reactors. While foams improve heat transfer, they

high reactant flow rates are necessary [15–17]. Another challenge is the
mechanical stability of the foam structure, a high porosity may cause
deformation or collapse over time, especially under high pressure and
temperature conditions [18–20]. Addressing these challenges requires
further optimization of foam materials and configurations to ensure
consistent performance and longevity in industrial applications [20].
Monolith supports have garnered significant attention in recent
years. They consist of continuous solid structures with numerous chan￾nels running in parallel. This design not only reduces pressure drop
but also improves flow uniformity and temperature distribution across
the channels [21]. Among these designs, non-homogeneous core/ring
monolith substrates featuring different cell densities in the core and
periphery have shown great potential for enhancing flow control. By
adjusting geometrical variables, these configurations may further po￾tentially optimize temperature profiles and reactor performance [22,
23].
Mixing pellet sizes has been shown to significantly influence flow
behavior in industrial processes. When particles vary in size, predicting
the pressure drop becomes more challenging due to complex fluid￾particle interactions, particularly when the size ratio between particles
is large. The Ergun equation is traditionally employed to estimate
pressure drop in packed beds but shows limitations when applied to
systems with polydisperse particle size distributions [24,25]. Despite
these complexities, fluid dynamics research often assumes uniform
particle sizes, as it remains a valid approximation for many practical
applications.
In the analysis of packed bed reactors, pressure drop is a key factor
influenced by flow distribution and plays a critical role in reducing
operating costs. The Ergun equation [26] is the most widely used model
for predicting pressure drop in packed beds [27], though some studies
highlight its limitations. Specifically, it has been shown to overpre￾dict pressure drop when the particle Reynolds number (Re𝑝
) exceeds
300 [27]. To improve its accuracy, several modifications have been
proposed. Some researchers have adjusted the equation’s constants to
fit specific experimental conditions, as seen in the work of Macdonald
et al. [28], KTA [29], and Ozahi et al. [30]. Others have incorporated
additional terms to account for porosity, such as in the models by
Rose [31] and Gibilaro et al. [32]. Wall effects also become significant
in smaller reactors, where the ratio of tube diameter to particle size
is reduced, prompting models that address these effects, such as those
by Montillet [33] and Harrison et al. [34]. Although no consensus or
general formula has been established across these models, the Ergun
equation remains the standard in predictive studies involving packed
beds [1,4,35], and it is also adopted for the purposes of this work.
A more detailed description and analysis of these models, along with
others, can be found in [27].
The Ergun equation [26] consists of two terms. The first is linear
and corresponds to the Kozeny–Carman equation, which applies to low
Reynolds numbers (Re𝑝 < 10). The second is quadratic, known as the
Burke-Plummer equation, and is relevant at high Reynolds numbers
(Re𝑝 > 1000) [36]. These terms capture different physical phenomena.
The linear term accounts for pressure losses due to viscous forces,
while the quadratic one describes losses because of the inertial forces.
Together, they model the cumulative pressure drop resulting from
solid-fluid interactions across a wide range of flow regimes [26]. The
complete equation is expressed as follows:

Recent studies have proposed alternative packed-bed reactor con￾figurations, such as using square channels or operating with a small
tube-to-particle diameter ratio, to better understand transport phe￾nomena and mitigate thermal hotspots in catalytic processes [37,38].
These approaches, however, often require refinements to Ergun-like
correlations, particularly by modifying inertial terms or accounting for
wall effects in non-standard packings. Together, these cases underscore
that the Ergun framework is still evolving through continuous refine￾ments, supporting that pressure-drop modeling in complex packed-bed
geometries remains an interesting topic for the community.
Selecting an appropriate pressure drop model is essential for de￾veloping a flow distribution model in dual-zone reactors, such as the
one proposed by Reinao et al. [23]. This approach relies on two key
principles: (i) mass balance, where the inlet flow is divided between
the core and ring regions, and (ii) backpressure equilibrium, where
the flow adjusts such that the pressure drop across both regions be￾comes balanced. In this configuration, a region with lower backpressure
receives more flow, increasing its pressure drop until equilibrium is
achieved. However, the application of these concepts to packed bed
reactors remains largely unexplored. Driven by the need for efficient
and reliable catalytic processes, this study aims to address the gap
in understanding flow distribution in non-homogeneous packed bed
reactors.
This investigation hypothesizes that a strategic mixture of pellet
sizes in the core and peripheral (ring) regions, as depicted in Fig.
1, may effectively control flow distribution and, in turn, regulate the
temperature profile. This configuration targets preventing central hot
spots in the bed, where temperatures tend to be highest due to the
distance from external cooling. While traditional hot spots due to
local irregularities may still occur within the core or ring regions,
this approach manipulates flow distribution to reduce the likelihood of
overheating in the bed’s center, enhancing reactor performance by tai￾loring flow resistance in each region. However, designing such reactors
through preliminary calculations requires accurate predictions of the
residence time of reactants in each zone, a challenge that relies on the
absence of engineering models capable of predicting flow distribution
in dual-zone packed beds. Despite the maturity of Ergun-like models,
surprisingly, no explicitly available models address 𝑐𝑜𝑟𝑒-𝑟𝑖𝑛𝑔 concentric
configurations such as the one covered in this study. The lack of a
dedicated framework for dual-zone packed beds presents a gap for
systematic exploration of such configurations, particularly in terms of
explicitly quantifying the influence of local parameters such as particle
size on, for example, flow distribution. This study addresses this gap
by developing the first predictive model tailored for these systems,
providing a relevant tool for preliminary reactor design. The proposed
model aims to enable more accurate flow predictions and support the
optimization of catalytic reactor performance.
In the presented context, the impact of varying pellet sizes between
the core and ring regions of the bed is investigated using CFD. This
configuration aims to create disparities in apparent permeability and
inertial resistance, enabling controlled flow distribution. A reactor-scale
model of the core/ring bed is employed to obtain flow distribution
data and compare it with predictions from a theoretical model. First,
the computational model is described along with a parametric study
evaluating the effects of pellet size, inlet Reynolds number, and core
size. Then, a theoretical model, based on the backpressure equilibrium
and Ergun equation, is developed to capture the balance in pressure
drop between regions with different particle sizes, facilitating a stable
distribution of flow. The results from both the computational and
theoretical approaches are then compared to assess the accuracy and
applicability of the proposed model.

2. Computational model
This section presents the development and implementation of the
computational model used to analyze flow distribution and pressure
drop in dual-zone packed beds.
2.1. Description of the domain
The computational domain was set to represent the dual-zone
packed bed configuration from Fig. 1, allowing for variations in flow
resistance between the core and ring regions to evaluate their impact
on flow distribution. A schematic of the axisymmetric section used to
generate the domain is shown in Fig. 2. The domain is divided into
three sections: an inlet space before the pellet bed, an outlet part after
the bed, and a middle section containing the packed bed. The lengths
of these sections were L𝑖𝑛 = 1000 mm, L𝑜𝑢𝑡 = 20 mm, and L𝑠 = 150 mm,
respectively. The overall system radius (R𝑡
) was 35 mm. The packed bed
consisted of spherical pellets with a uniform void fraction of 0.4, which
is, in general terms, independent of the particle diameter [39,40].
The core and ring regions feature different apparent permeability and
inertial resistance due to variations in particle size.
The bed is modeled using a continuum approach, where the sub￾strate is treated as a homogeneous porous medium with a represen￾tative apparent permeability and porosity. This approach simplifies
the simulation by significantly reducing computational cost, making it
suitable for systematic research. It leverages the axial symmetry of the
domain to focus on macroscopic flow behaviors rather than particle￾scale interactions. While a DEM-CFD coupled model could provide
more detailed insights into individual particle interactions [41], it goes
beyond the scope of the current investigation, taking into account
that the primary objective of the study is to analyze the overall flow
distribution, for which the continuum approach is well-established and
validated in packed bed reactor analysis.

The problem was implemented using the commercial CFD code
ANSYS Fluent 2023R2 [42], which employs the finite volume method.
Atmospheric air was used as the working fluid, with a constant density
of 1.225 kg/m3 and a constant viscosity of 1.7894 × 10−5 Pa⋅s. The
top boundary was defined as a no-slip wall for momentum, while
the bottom boundary, representing the tube’s centerline, was set as
a symmetry axis. The inlet velocity had a flat profile and varied in
velocity magnitude across different runs, while the outlet boundary
condition was set to a prescribed pressure of 1 bar.
For interpolating, a second-order upwind scheme was used for
momentum, and the pressure–velocity coupling was solved using a
pseudo-transient, fully coupled approach. The convergence criteria for
every run was having scaled residuals below 10−4

2.3. CFD model validation and grid independence
This section is intended to validate the CFD framework, providing
a spatially distributed reference for comparison with the proposed
one-dimensional theoretical model. The grid independence study was
performed by comparing the pressure drop across different meshes,
using a particle diameter of 1 mm and an inlet Reynolds number
(Re𝑖𝑛) of 10 000. The grid, fully composed of rectangular elements,
was progressively refined, maintaining a constant aspect ratio of 1:2
between the vertical and horizontal dimensions of the control volumes,
which aligns with the flow direction. The results are presented in Fig.
3. The difference in pressure drop between the second-to-last mesh
(329 940 control volumes) and the finest mesh (517 725 control volumes)
was only 0.04%. Since this difference is negligible, the mesh with
329 940 control volumes was selected for further simulations.
Given the absence of models for flow distribution in non-homogen￾eous fixed beds in the literature, the developed computational model
was validated by comparing its pressure drop predictions with those
obtained from Ergun’s equation (Eq. (1)) for flow through a packed
bed, as described in Section 1.

3. Results and discussion
This section summarizes the main results obtained. As described
by the Ergun equation, the principal variables affecting pressure drop,
and consequently flow distribution, inside a packed bed are the in￾let (superficial) velocity, represented by Re𝑖𝑛, and particle diameter
combination. These variables were selected for the parametric study,
along with core size, in alignment with previous investigations on dual￾cell density monolith systems [23]. Three values were chosen for Re𝑖𝑛:
100, 1000, and 10 000, which correspond to particle Reynolds numbers
(Re𝑝
) ranging from 1 to 500, covering a broad spectrum of values
commonly reported in the literature [27,33,48]. The particle diameter
in the ring region was held constant at 2 mm, while the core particle
size was varied to obtain relative particle diameters (𝐷𝑝,𝑐∕𝐷𝑝,𝑟) ranging
from 0.50 to 1.50, with increments of 0.25. Core size, defined as the
percentage of the bed cross-sectional area assigned to the core region,
was varied from 25% to 100% in increments of 25%. It is important to
note that when the core size is 0% and 100%, the system corresponds to
a homogeneous packed bed with the particle diameter corresponding to
𝐷𝑝,𝑟 and 𝐷𝑝,𝑐 respectively. By combining all possible parameter values
mentioned, a total of 60 simulations were performed, as listed in Table
1.

4. Conclusions
The flow distribution in a non-homogeneous fixed bed with dual
pellet sizes in the core and ring regions was successfully investigated
using a computational model. The results indicate that the fraction of
flow passing through the core is influenced by several factors, including
core size, relative pellet diameter, and tube Reynolds number.
Larger core sizes result in higher flow percentages through the core,
as the increased area reduces resistance compared to the ring region.
At lower Reynolds numbers, the flow through the core is smaller, but
it increases with higher Reynolds numbers, suggesting that higher flow
rates promote more flow through the central region. This effect is
more pronounced for larger core sizes, where the differences in flow
percentage between Reynolds numbers are more significant.
The relative diameter of the pellets (core over ring) also plays a
crucial role in flow distribution. When the relative diameter is less than
1.00, the flow through the core is reduced. As the relative diameter
increases beyond 1.00, the flow through the core increases, although
this effect diminishes at higher Reynolds numbers. A relative diameter
of 1.00 leads to uniform flow distribution, where the percentage of flow
through the core matches the core size.
A predictive model is developed to describe the flow distribution
between the core and ring regions, accounting for both viscous and
inertial resistances. The model is expressed as a quadratic equation,
with coefficients dependent on the properties of the system, including
core size, Reynolds number, pellet diameters, and fluid properties
(density and viscosity).
Comparison of the proposed model with CFD simulation data shows
excellent agreement across several core sizes, Reynolds numbers, and
pellet diameters. This comparison suggests the model’s potential for
optimizing the design of packed beds with dual pellet sizes to achieve
improved flow distribution. Future experimental validation is recom￾mended to confirm the model’s accuracy under a broader range of
operating conditions in non-ideal scenarios. Since flow distribution
dictates heat generation and transport in reactive systems, the exten￾sion of this work to include temperature distribution and conversion
analysis is a logical next step. Future studies should also integrate these
aspects to provide a comprehensive understanding of dual-zone reactor
performance with significant thermal effects.
