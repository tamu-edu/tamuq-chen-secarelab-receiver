A B S T R A C T
Solar tower plants are predicted to take on a significant role in the ongoing global energy transition. The open
volumetric air receiver technology can provide an alternative to molten salt receivers due to its proven and
effective storage capabilities. The key component of this technology are porous absorber structures which are
responsible for the absorption of the solar irradiation. Research of porous structures has consistently shown
that 3D-shaped absorber designs offer the highest efficiency potential. The realization of such designs is made
possible by newer manufacturing techniques.
In this publication, experimental results for two groups of new volumetric absorbers are presented. The
StepRec absorber is manufactured via ceramic 3D screen printing, its characteristic step-pin sections allow for
deep penetration of the solar irradiation. Additionally, metallic foil absorbers were evaluated, they exhibit
particularly high specific surface areas due to low wall thicknesses. The new absorbers were tested for flux
densities up to 650 kW
m2 and air exit temperatures up to 735 °C. The experiments successfully confirmed the new
absorbers’ great potential: They outperformed the HiTRec reference, a ceramic honeycomb structure, for all
air temperatures. At 650 °C, thermal efficiencies up to 91.8% were recorded, an improvement of 6.5%-points
w.r.t. to the HiTRec result.
1. Introduction
CSP and especially solar power tower plants are expected to play
an important role in the ongoing global energy transition [1]. With its
proven and effective storage, the open volumetric air receiver is a real
alternative to the currently prevailing molten salt receiver technology,
as was already stated 1993 in the Second Generation Study [2]. Advan￾tages of the air receiver technology lie in the simplicity of design, the
robustness of operation and the virtually unlimited temperature range
of air as heat transfer medium. Therefore, since many years, continuing
effort has been put into the development of the key component of this
technology, porous structures as volumetric absorbers of concentrated
sunlight. A comprehensive overview of this technology development
can be found in the detailed review by Ávila-Marín [3].

1.1. First generation of absorber structures
Volumetric absorbers allow the radiation to penetrate deep into
their volume to be absorbed and transferred as heat to the parallel
gas flow by the large inner surface. Unsurprisingly, woven or knitted
meshes of thin metal wires from temperature resistant steel alloys
dominated the first steps of this development [3]. Fricker [4] shall be
mentioned here, who pioneered with a seven-layer wire mesh absorber
made of 0.71 mm heat-resisting CrNi steel wire in 1985 which lead to
the 2.5 MWth TSA receiver tested in the Spanish Plataforma Solar (PSA)
in 1993, composed of hexagonal absorber modules packed with rolled
layers of knitted Inconel® wire [5].
Further structures examined in the early phase of development
were either regular channel-like geometries or isotropic reticulated

1.2. Variation of structural geometry along the flow direction
The structures of this first phase of development have in common
that they almost exclusively exhibit uniform geometric parameters
throughout the volume, i.e. single wall size, channel width, mesh size
etc. But, some first attempts can be found in literature to alter the
design of the frontal structure, with the purpose to change geometric,
optical or heat-transfer related parameters in flow direction.
Bechtel tested a wire mesh absorber composed of several layers of
thin 0.1 mm wire followed by layers with thicker 0.2 mm wires [11].
DLR and Hoechst CeramTec developed and tested a ceramic channel
structure composed of foils and rods where in the front section every
second foil and every second rod were shortened [9] (cf. Fig. 1). Fend
et al. [12] tested the combination of a thin front layer of an 80 ppi
ceramic foam covering a thicker main absorber of 20 ppi foam of the
same material. All these attempts tried to reduce thermal losses by
offering a higher porosity at the front resulting in deeper penetration
of the radiation into the volume of the structure.
After these early, rather exploratory design variations, attempts
were made to vary the geometry of porous absorber structures more
purposefully. Roldán et al. [13] used a 2-dimensional CFD-model for
the comparison of ceramic honeycombs of varying porosity in radial
and axial direction with reference to a structure of constant porosity,
finding decreasing porosity in flow direction to be the best performing
structure. Ávila-Marín et al. [14,15] experimentally investigated the
performance of gradual porosity absorbers consisting of combinations
of wire meshes with different geometrical properties, concluding that
double layer meshes with the front layer having the highest possible
porosity and the second layer having the highest possible specific
surface area give the best results. Zaversky et al. [16] numerically and
experimentally evaluated single and multi-layer ceramic foams as solar

1.3. Structural design adapted to manufacturing processes
This work is based on the idea of incorporating the possibilities
and limitations of the chosen manufacturing methods and materials
into the design process. Therefore, specialists from the manufacturing
companies were involved in the development of the specific structural
designs. The work focused on two production technologies:
• innovative ceramic honeycomb structures manufactured by screen
printing out of SiSiC
• channel structures made from pairs of planar and corrugated
metal sheets, similar to those presented by Pabst et al. [18]
A new simulation model was applied as part of the design process. This
model and related simulation results are described in detail in Broeske
et al. [19]. From the most promising geometries, several design varia￾tions were produced and tested under close-to-real experimental con￾ditions in DLR’s large solar simulator Synlight® [20]. As a benchmark,
the state of the art of the so-called HiTRec technology was used: an
extruded honeycomb monolith made from SiSiC ceramics with a 2.0 mm
channel width and a 0.8 mm wall thickness as it is deployed in the
Solar Tower in Jülich (Germany), where the technology of the open
volumetric receiver is demonstrated as a power plant system since
2009 [21].
In the next section the chosen manufacturing methods and the
implications and limitations on the design process are presented. After￾wards, the test setup and the experimental parameters are described to
assess the conditions of the measurement procedure. The experimental
results are presented and discussed in the subsequent chapter. Finally,
the findings are summarized and conclusions drawn.
2. Absorber structures
2.1. Screen printed ceramics
The first new absorber design is manufactured via a 3D screen
printing process developed by the company Exentis Group AG [22].
As with 2D screen printing, 3D screen printing is based on constructing
a stencil on a fine mesh screen and pushing a special paste through
the printing screen to create an imprint of the design on the surface
beneath. To extend this concept into 3D parts, this process is repeated
continuously while the printing screen is moved upwards. This causes
the printed part to ‘‘grow’’ layer by layer. Depending on paste and
printing parameters, each layer is usually between 7 − 40 μm thick. For
very precise and filigree parts, a small layer increment is preferred
whereas larger increments lead to faster growing parts and therefore
a decrease in process time. To change the geometry in the z-axis
direction, a switch of the printing screen is necessary. In theory, using a
new screen for each layer would be possible for maximum geometrical
flexibility, but this is not practical when taking economic feasibility into
account. Therefore, the volumetric receiver proposed by Capuano et al.
[10] with sloped pins was adjusted to better fit the 3D screen printing

technology: The sloped pins were replaced by three ‘‘step’’-like sections
(cf. Fig. 2) giving name to the so called StepRec design.
Despite the simple concept of 3D screen printing, the actual printing
of 3D parts is quite complex. It requires highly developed machines,
paste, screens and printing parameters, all of which must be fine￾tuned to each other. The flexibility in material selection is possible
due to applying pastes, which usually combine the desired material
in powder form with binder and other additives. In case of the solar
absorbers, a specialized paste containing silicon carbide and carbon
powder as the base material, binders to realize sufficient green body
stability, a fluid carrier medium and rheology additives was developed.
The additives serve to optimize the rheological behavior of the 3D
screen printing paste. Usually, a shear-thinning behavior is desirable
to prevent unwanted seep when the sieve is flooded with paste, while
still allowing for smooth flow when moved by the squeegee.
The StepRec design consists of five different layers, two for the
base with offset square channels and the three step-like pin layers. The
channels itself are no challenge to print. A challenge arises from the
fact that the channels are offset on top of each other, since a new
layer can only be built on an underlying foundation. Depending on
the existing structure and gap width, it is possible to build an arched
bridge, because the paste sticks to the before printed structures and
narrows the gap with each layer. Unfortunately, this often reduces the
stability of the green body and therefore can lead to problems with
increasing gap width and the height on top. The better solution in this
case was printing layer one and layer two to five separately and then
join them together with a small amount of paste as adhesive that was
printed on layer one.
After being printed, the absorber samples are sintered in a combined
thermal process [22]. During this process, the organic binder materials
are removed, before the solar absorber is infiltrated with molten silicon.
The additional silicon fills remaining pore structures within the ab￾sorber material and partially reacts with free carbon to secondary SiC.

2.2. Soldered metal sheets
The evaluated metallic solar absorbers were manufactured by the
company Vitesco Technologies Emitec, utilizing the company’s long￾standing experience and know-how from the automotive exhaust gas af￾tertreatment industry. These absorbers, hereafter referred to as EmiRec
absorbers, are made of a modified high-temperature iron-based al￾loy specially developed for corrosive high-temperature applications
(1.4767 MOD - iron–chromium–aluminum alloy). The material has
the property of forming a protective aluminum oxide layer during
operation at high temperatures, which prevents oxidation of the inner
material core.
The matrix structure of the EmiRec absorbers consists of thin cut￾to-length sheet foils, which are stacked alternately from a corrugated
layer and a smooth layer to form a package and inserted into a square
tube (cf. Fig. 3). At defined contact points between the sheet foils and
between the matrix package and the square tube, a material-to-material
bond is produced by means of the vacuum brazing technology. The
connections between the sheet foils and between the matrix package
and the tube provide mechanical strength while at the same time being
flexible enough to compensate for stresses in the material and prevent
plastic deformation under high thermal shock loads. High thermal
shock loads occur especially during cloud passages when the heliostats
are suddenly shaded.
The square design of the EmiRec solar absorbers makes optimum use
of the available installation space in the receiver field on the solar tower
and can be individually adapted to the boundary conditions of different
solar power plants. The high-temperature material used is processed in
thin sheet metal strips, which are characterized in particular by good
heating behavior, low heat capacity and very good axial and radial
thermal conductivity. The low wall thickness of the metal foils allows
a high porosity despite a large specific surface in direct comparison
with the state of the art SiSiC absorber. For the tests in the Synlight
facility, the solar absorbers were pre-oxidized in a high-temperature
furnace, which darkens the surface of the high-temperature material.
The darkened surface improves the absorptivity and thus increases the
thermal efficiency of the solar absorbers. During operation on a solar
power plant, the reaction with oxygen takes place and forms the oxide
layer after a short period of operation.
In upstream production tests, various design approaches for increas￾ing the inlet porosity of the metallic EmiRec solar absorbers were
investigated. Eventually, it was decided to shorten or retract the smooth
layer and set it flush with the gas outlet side (cf. Fig. 3, image A). By
shortening the smooth layer in the matrix package, the axial porosity
can be adjusted in relation to the angle of incidence of the solar
irradiation. As part of the Synlight test campaign, a number of designs
were evaluated to test various manufacturing characteristics. However,
this publication will focus on the three most promising designs: The first
two, EmiRec 300 cpsi and EmiRec 600 cpsi, are standard designs with
the smooth layers shortened by 10 mm and differ only regarding the
channel density. For the third design, EmiRec 600 cpsi MOD, not only
the smooth layers were shortened, but every second corrugated layer
was also shortened by 10 mm. This change further increases the inlet
porosity.

3.3. Test procedure
The main test campaign was carried out at the high-flux solar
simulator facility Synlight® of the DLR in Jülich, Germany. The core
of the campaign then consisted of a series of test days, where data
was recorded for one absorber configuration set and one flux density
distribution during one test day (cf. Section 3.4).
The goal was to determine the thermal efficiency of the different
absorbers at steady state operation. To achieve this, a series of different
temperature target levels were evaluated over the course of one test day
in a ‘‘pyramid’’-pattern from low to high to low. The temperature levels
were controlled via the total air mass flow through the test rig. The ac￾tual outlet temperatures varied between the sixteen absorber samples,
in part because of their different efficiencies. Hence, the temperature
levels served only as target values. For each level, the actual evaluation
period started once the temperature gradients of all absorbers dropped
and stayed below 2
K

periods was then also set as ten minutes, during this time at least
three infrared images were recorded. The air exit temperatures were
recorded continuously by the test rig. In total, the highest recorded

3.5. Uncertainty analysis
The uncertainty of the conducted experiments has been analyzed
based on the methodology described in the ‘‘GUM: Guide to the Ex￾pression of Uncertainty in Measurement’’ [25].
Based on the manufacturer specifications for the thermocouples
(type N, TMH [26]), the maximum temperature uncertainty can be ex￾pected to be below ±3 °C. Compared to the other sources of uncertainty,
this is considered as negligible.
The general uncertainty of the FMAS measurement system under
ideal conditions is up to three percent [24], a more conservative uncer￾tainty 𝛿𝐹𝑀𝐴𝑆 of up to six percent is assumed here based on experience
from other DLR experiments. Deviations between the expected position￾ing and the actual positioning of the absorber samples within the test
rig were considered as an additional uncertainty for the evaluation of
the flux density 𝐼𝑎𝑣𝑔 and the emission losses ̇𝑞𝑙𝑜𝑠𝑠,𝑒𝑚𝑖. These uncertainties
𝛿𝑝𝑜𝑠,𝑠𝑜𝑙 and 𝛿𝑝𝑜𝑠,𝑒𝑚𝑖 were estimated via two sensitivity studies testing
differences of up to 1 cm in any direction. Regarding the emission
losses, the positioning of the IR camera relative to the test rig and the
general accuracy of the IR camera were discussed as further sources of
uncertainty, but their influence could not be quantified.
The evaluation process of the solar-weighted absorptivity is specif￾ically designed to reduce random errors (cf. Section 3.2). As an es￾timator of the random errors (e.g. geometric sample impurities), the
solar-weighted absorptivity evaluated based on the averaged measure￾ments was compared to the absorptivity values calculated from each
individual measurement. This comparison resulted in an uncertainty
𝛿𝑎𝑏𝑠,𝑟𝑎𝑛𝑑 of up to one percentage point. Systemic deviations between
the measurements with the spectrometer and the irradiation tests in the
Synlight were considered w.r.t. the irradiation direction. The spectrom￾eter measures the sample reflectivity for a shallow incident angle of
eight to ten degrees. For the Synlight facility, higher incident angles are
possible depending on which lamps are used. This uncertainty 𝛿𝑎𝑏𝑠,𝑑𝑖𝑟
was quantified based on a comparison of raytracing simulations, this is
explained in Appendix A. Deviations of the spectral light distribution
between the Synlight lamps and spectrometer evaluation were consid￾ered, but deemed negligible as the Synlight facility was specifically
designed to mimic real solar irradiation conditions.

4. Results and discussion
4.1. Solar-weighted absorptivity
Needed for the evaluation of the absorber efficiencies, the solar￾weighted absorptivity of each sample was determined with the method￾ology described in Section 3.2. The solar weighted absorptivity results
for the different absorber samples are presented in Table 3. Three
separate measurements were averaged for each absorber sample. In
Table 3, the potential of the new 3D-shaped absorbers is already visible.
The two StepRec samples absorb 6.1 percentage points more of the
incoming irradiation than the reference HiTRec absorber, while the
EmiRec designs absorb up to 5.1 percentage points more. During previ￾ous test campaigns, darkening of the metallic EmiRec absorbers caused
by oxidation was observed. Because this proved beneficial w.r.t. the
absorptivity, the manufacturer decided to forcibly oxidize the new ab￾sorber modules before this test campaign. The coarser 300 cpsi samples
outperform the finer 600 cpsi channel density, because the increasing
channel density (combined with the fixed wall strength) leads to a
decreasing porosity. However, the additional retraction of every second
corrugated layer allows the EmiRec 600 cpsi MOD design to reach the
absorptivity level of the coarse absorbers.

between two to four times as large as for the HiTRec absorber. The
StepRec design also has a higher average porosity (78%) and a spec.

for a much deeper penetration of the solar irradiation. Overall, this
enables the new designs to reach the measured high efficiency levels
and outperform the state-of-the-art absorber.

5. Conclusion and outlook
An experimental test campaign testing new 3D-shaped volumetric
absorbers has been successfully conducted at DLR’s high-flux solar
simulator facility Synlight. The new designs included ceramic 3D screen
printed absorbers manufactured by the company Exentis Technology
GmbH and metallic foil absorbers from Vitesco Technologies Emitec
GmbH. The new 3D-shaped porous absorber geometries showed the
great potential for their use as part of open volumetric receivers:
At air exit temperatures of 650 °C, (rectified) absorber efficiencies of
up to 91.8% were recorded for the new designs. This represents an
improvement of up to 6.5 percentage points compared to the state￾of-the-art, ceramic HiTRec absorbers, which were also evaluated as
reference. The absorber samples were tested for average flux densities
of up to 650 kW
m2
(second campaign) and air exit temperatures of up to
725 °C. The shown efficiency improvement of the new absorbers also
extended to lower temperatures and the solar-weighted absorptivity,
which was determined via auxiliary measurements. As the next step,
a economical analysis of the new absorber designs will be conducted
within the associated research project.

EmiRec 600 cpsi MOD variant, every second corrugated layer was also
shortened by 10 mm to further increase the porosity at the irradiated
front.
