A new model for the pressure drop in dual-cell density monoliths:
Experimental validation and CFD

A B S T R A C T
This study presents the first experimental validation of a theoretical model for predicting pressure drop in dual￾cell density monoliths, a configuration aimed at optimizing flow distribution in industrial catalytic reactors.
3D-printed monoliths, featuring two concentric regions — core and ring — were employed to systematically
investigate variations in core size and relative permeability between regions. The monoliths were tested
over a wide range of Reynolds numbers using a custom-built experimental rig. The results demonstrate
strong agreement with the model’s predictions and confirm its accuracy in estimating pressure drop under
different configurations. Complementary CFD simulations further support the experimental results and provide
additional insights into the effects of model assumptions on pressure drop predictions. The study validates the
model as a useful tool for engineering applications, offering valuable insights for the design and optimization
of non-homogeneous monolith substrates in industrial catalytic systems.
1. Introduction
Catalytic processes are important to numerous industrial applica￾tions, ranging from chemical manufacturing to environmental protec￾tion. These processes play a critical role in improving the efficiency
and sustainability of chemical plants by optimizing throughput and
reducing energy consumption [1]. While packed bed reactors have
been the traditional choice, they suffer from high pressure drop, poor
flow distribution, and challenges in thermal gradient management [2].
These limitations, especially in large-scale operations, have led to the
development of structured reactors, where the geometry, ranging from
particle size to reactor scale, is precisely defined by design [3].
Among the structured substrates, monolith reactors have emerged as
a promising alternative to traditional packed beds, particularly in high￾flow applications. These reactors consist of a structured, honeycomb￾like array of parallel channels, which serve as catalyst supports [4].
In packed beds, the pressure drop is typically modeled using the
Ergun equation, which accounts for the interactions between the fluid
phase and the pellets, leading to both viscous and inertial losses [5]
which explains greater pressure drop while increasing flow. However,
studies have shown that polydisperse pellet beds, common in indus￾trial reactors, can increase flow resistance, affecting pressure drop,
and Ergun predictions underestimate it [6,7]. Moreover, poor flow
distribution can create hot spots, particularly in exothermic reactions,
as low flow areas concentrate reactants, accelerating conversion rate

The use of monoliths as catalytic support offers several improve￾ments, including a significantly lower pressure drop and a higher
surface-to-volume ratio compared to packed beds, making them es￾pecially suitable for processes requiring high throughput with low
pressure drop [14]. Moreover, heat and mass transfer for heterogeneous
reactions in monolith reactors is improved by the higher specific area
and more even flow distribution, leading to an increased catalyst
usage [15,16]. Consequently, monoliths are now widely used in auto￾motive catalytic converters for automobile exhaust treatment and are
increasingly considered in various chemical synthesis systems [17,18];
Some examples are CO2 methanation [19,20], methanol synthesis [21–
23], methane reforming [24–26] and destruction of volatile organic
compounds (VOC) [27].
Monolith channels can be manufactured in different shapes, such
as circular, square, triangular, hexagonal, and sinusoidal, among oth￾ers [28]. The catalyst is dispersed within the washcoat that is coated
onto the inner surface of the channels [29], so the washcoat is a
secondary support that provides for better adherence and improved
dispersion of the active phase [30]. Some geometric parameters deter￾mine the monolith design. In particular, for square-shaped monolith

Typically, monoliths are also characterized by their cell density (𝑁),

Despite the stated benefits, there is still room for improvement
in monolith design, particularly in optimizing flow distribution. In
catalytic converters, the primary application of monoliths, flow dis￾tribution has been extensively studied because the flow that comes
from the inlet diffuser tends to concentrate at the center, leading
to maldistribution [32]. For an homogeneous monolith with constant
cell density, any poor distribution of the incoming flow will tend to
persist, as there is no internal characteristic capable of redistributing
the flow more efficiently. This results in uncontrolled variations in
flow rates and residence times. It has been reported that in such
cases, the catalyst within traditional monoliths is only partially utilized,
resulting in reduced conversion efficiency and a shortened operational
lifespan [33–35]. Important variables, such as pressure drop, are also
affected [36,37]. These points could be addressed by manipulating
the flow distribution within the monolith geometry. Another factor to
consider is temperature control, crucial in monolith applications involv￾ing exothermic reactions, which often require external cooling [21].
Despite the manufacturing challenges, highly thermally conductive
ceramic and metallic substrates have become commercially available in
recent years, which mitigates this restriction. Examples include silicon
carbide and titanium aluminate honeycombs used in DPF/SCR filters
and corrugated Fe-Cr-Al foil metallic monoliths used in Fischer–Tropsch
reactors and high-flux methanation [38]. Recent trials with 3D-printed
Ni/SiC monoliths demonstrate thermal stability and low pressure drop
in methane reforming at 900 ◦C [26] supporting the possibility of
manufacturing conductive structures on an industrial scale. A viable
alternative involves controlling the flow distribution, directing more
flow through the outer region of the monolith, convection is enhanced
in that area, improving heat transfer and reducing the risk of hot
spots and thermal gradients. In that line, the work from Kane and
Northrop [39] reports experiments demonstrating improved heat trans￾fer without compromising conversion efficiency in a non-homogeneous
concentric substrate, highlighting the advantages of such designs.
Other researchers have also highlighted the potential to manipulate
reactor performance using non-homogeneous substrates, particularly
dual-cell density monoliths, where the cell density varies radially to
form two concentric zones with distinct geometric characteristics [40].
This approach has since been further developed by results from our
group [41]. As shown in Fig. 1, the inner zone is referred to as the core,
while the outer one is called the ring. The difference in cell density
between these zones varies the viscous resistance to flow, resulting
in higher flow through the more permeable region. This distribution
can be particularly suitable for temperature control in exothermic,
externally cooled systems.
Early models for homogeneous monolith reactors, such as those
based on the Hagen–Poiseuille equation, assumed fully developed lam￾inar flow within uniform channels and provided reliable predictions
for homogeneous monoliths under idealized conditions. Due to com￾putational constraints, an extensively used approach is to model the
monolith as a continuous medium. This allows for studying fluid dy￾namics at a larger scale, eliminating the need to explicitly solve the
equations governing phenomena at the channel scale and within the
washcoat, which is computationally expensive, even when only a single
channel is considered [42,43]. To accurately represent the substrate,

physical channels [37,44]. To derive the source term in the momentum

2. Experimental setup
2.1. Monolith manufacture
Dual-cell density monoliths were digitally modeled using Autodesk
Inventor Professional 2023 [47]. For all the cases, the channels were
square-shaped due to their ease of design, simplified modeling, and
manufacturability through 3D printing, which ensures a more accurate
reproduction of geometric parameters. Changes in channel shape would
adjust the permeability through the shape factor 𝐶, with the model re￾maining applicable. The core geometric parameters, namely hydraulic
diameter and wall thickness, were kept constant across all experiments.
That is, the inner zone apparent permeability remained constant. It
was calculated by using Eq. (2). Variations were introduced only in
the core size, which ranged from 0.25 to 0.50, and in the apparent
permeability of the ring zone. As a result, the permeability ratio (𝛼𝑐∕𝛼𝑟
)
moved from 1.5 to 2.0. These values correspond to the ones observed
in typical monoliths, as axial permeabilities range between 1.2×10−8 to
3.1 × 10−8 m2
, [37]. The selected flow area ratios (0.25, 0.50) represent
feasible design configurations for dual-zone monoliths, allowing for
a representative analysis of flow behavior. Four different monolith
configurations were designed for experimentation. Their details are
provided in Table 1.
The monoliths were 3D-printed in water-washable resin (Anycubic,
grey) with a DLP 3D printer (Creality, Halot-Sky) producing structures
150 mm in length and 68 mm in outer diameter. This material was
selected for its ease of fabrication and precision in reproducing complex
geometries. Given that the model focuses on flow behavior governed
by geometry, monoliths 3D-printed in resin were considered suitable
for this study. After printing, the models were washed with water to
remove excess resin, air-dried at ambient temperature, and cured under
UV light for 20 min. Fig. 2 shows an example of a digital dual-cell
density monolith and its 3D-printed counterpart.

without requiring the resolution of individual channel details.

There is substantial evidence in the literature that once turbulent
flow enters the monolith, and there is a sub-critical Reynolds number
inside of the channels, then turbulence quickly decays [53]. Based
on that, inside of the monolith, both turbulence kinetic energy and
specific dissipation ratio were set to zero. Despite that, the flow does
not become steady; instead, it becomes laminar unsteady in a sort of
pulsatile regime. Such a phenomenon has been previously investigated
by our group, reporting that it does not significantly affect the pressure
drop [54]. Therefore, assuming a steady laminar flow friction factor
still recognizes pulsating flow without violating mass conservation. It
is also known that turbulence can arise because of the flow leaving the
substrate [55]. However, this has only a minor effect on total pressure
drop and can be neglected for most of the cases [56,57].
The monolith itself was modeled as a continuous porous medium,

4. Results and discussion
This section presents the main results obtained from the experimen￾tal trials and the corresponding simulation runs. The analysis is divided
into two parts: pressure drop behavior and flow distribution through
the dual-cell density monolith. Both experimental and computational
findings are compared to assess the accuracy of the computational
model and its potential for practical applications.
4.1. Backpressure and flow distribution
Fig. 8 shows the pressure distribution for the section of the domain
close to the substrate for an inlet Reynolds number of 650. The figure
covers all the core sizes and apparent permeabilities under study.
Notably, in the monolith area, the pressure is mostly uniform in the
radial direction across the whole monolith, confirming the backpressure
equilibrium assumption between the core and ring zones. In addition,
it is observed from comparing Fig. 8(a) and (b) that the pressure drop
increases with a smaller core size due to the reduction of the size
of the more permeable region. Regarding the effect of the relative
permeability, the differences between Fig. 8(a) and (c) show that a
higher ratio or disparity leads to a higher pressure drop as well.
Similarly, the velocity profile within the same zone of tube is
displayed in Fig. 9. It is observed that in the monolith region, the
velocity magnitude profile seems constant at their own core and ring
values. At smaller core size values, the variation in flow distribution be￾comes more significant, increasing the velocity value through the core.
Likewise, at larger relative permeabilities, the flow tends to increase
through the more permeable region as the difference in resistance to
flow between the core and ring becomes more significant.
To further analyze the flow distribution, Fig. 10 reports the axial
velocity profile at the outlet of the substrate for a homogeneous mono￾lith (𝛼𝑐∕𝛼𝑟 = 1.00). It is compared to that from a dual-cell density
monolith with the core region being more permeable (𝛼𝑐∕𝛼𝑟 = 2.32) and
one with the ring being more permeable (𝛼𝑟∕𝛼𝑐 = 2.32), interchanging
their local permeabilities. The axial velocity profiles of the dual-cell
density monoliths mirror each other symmetrically, indicating that
the permeability distribution significantly affects the flow field at the
monolith exit. The more permeable region consistently drives more
flow, leading to significantly different outlet velocity profiles compared
to the homogeneous case.
4.2. Pressure drop model
As previously mentioned, the pressure drop through the monolith,
by equals the ring and core, can be modeled by using Eq. (7), which
depends on the geometrical parameters of the core and ring, flow rate
and the properties of the operating fluid. The equation was tested
against experimental and CFD data for different relative permeabilities,
core sizes and Rein. The results are summarized in Fig. 11.
The results in Fig. 11 show that the theoretical model closely follows
the trends observed in both the experimental and CFD data, particularly
at lower Reynolds numbers. This agreement evidences that the model
effectively captures the main factors influencing pressure drop, even in
configurations with varying core sizes and apparent permeabilities.
For the case of 𝜆𝑐=0.25 and 𝛼𝑐
/𝛼𝑟=1.39 (Fig. 11(a)), the experi￾mental and CFD results are in strong agreement, and the theoretical
model closely matches both datasets. Minor deviations appear when
increasing Reynolds numbers, where the theoretical model slightly
underestimates the pressure drop. A similar trend is observed for
𝜆𝑐=0.50 and 𝛼𝑐
/𝛼𝑟=2.32 (Fig. 11(c)), where the theoretical model again
performs well, but shows small discrepancies at higher Re. These results
suggest that the theoretical model is robust in predicting pressure
drop for small-core configurations, with only slight underestimations
at higher flow rates.
In the case of a larger core with lower apparent permeability
(Fig. 11(b)), the theoretical model also performs well, closely aligning
with both the experimental and CFD data across the full range of
Reynolds numbers. However, as the apparent permeability increases in
the larger-core configuration (Fig. 11(d)), the discrepancies between
the theoretical model and the experimental/CFD results become more
pronounced at higher Re. While the model still provides a reasonable
approximation, it tends to underestimate the pressure drop more visibly
as Reynolds numbers increase. The underestimation of the pressure
drop in the mentioned cases suggests that additional factors, such as
entrance and exit effects or more complex flow resistance mechanisms,
may become important in higher flow rate conditions.
All cases show a linear relationship between Reynolds number and
pressure drop, confirming that the structure of the proposed theo￾retical model is fundamentally correct. This linearity supports a core
assumption of the model that pressure drop is dominated by fully

However, slight variations in the slope between the experimental, CFD,

the physical monolith and the values assumed in both the theoretical

to minor manufacturing imperfections, which are typical in extruded
monoliths but less pronounced in 3D-printed models. In this study,
DPL (Digital Light Processing) 3D printing was used, which is known
for its superior precision compared to traditional extrusion methods.
Nevertheless, despite this fact, small manufacturing irregularities in
the monolith were still observed. Variations in channel sizes and wall
thicknesses, even within the same region, were detected when mea￾suring the physical 3D-printed monoliths. These imperfections may
have affected the permeability and contributed to the observed de￾viations. Additionally, the theoretical and CFD models assumed that
all channels were perfectly square. Meanwhile, some real channels are
partially truncated by the boundaries of the core region and the shell
of the monolith. Those boundary channels have a smaller hydraulic
diameter compared to regular square channels. This leads to a lower
local apparent permeability, which in turn increases the slope of the
pressure drop versus Reynolds number trend, while maintaining its
linear character. This does not affect the backpressure equilibrium
assumption; however, it may become more significant as the fraction
of channels with non-regular shapes increases.
Overall, the differences between the model and the experiments
remained small. The marked alignment between the experimental and
CFD results with the theoretical model across all cases suggests the
reliability of both the experimental setup and the CFD approach, further
validating the proposed model for pressure drop in dual-cell density
monoliths.
As an additional analysis, the results presented in Fig. 12 further
emphasize the usefulness of the proposed model for predicting pressure
drop in dual-cell density monoliths. As shown in both plots, the pres￾sure drop for dual-cell configurations may significantly deviate from
that of homogeneous monoliths, whether assuming the permeability
of the core (black dashed line) or the ring (red dashed line). This
discrepancy is particularly pronounced in cases of high apparent perme￾ability (Fig. 12(b)), where the experimentally measured pressure drop
(red squares and black circles) may lie substantially distant from the
predictions for homogeneous monoliths. In both low and high apparent
relative permeability cases, neither the core nor the ring permeability
alone is sufficient to capture the pressure drop behavior of the dual-cell
structure, as the pressure drop for dual-cell monoliths results from the
complex interaction between the core and ring regions together with
the backpressure equilibrium. These findings highlight the limitations
of existing models that assume a single permeability and emphasize
the proposed model’s reliability, specifically designed to account for
the non-homogeneous nature of dual-cell density monoliths. Therefore,
this model can accurately predict pressure drop in such configurations,
which opens opportunities for further monolith shape optimization.
